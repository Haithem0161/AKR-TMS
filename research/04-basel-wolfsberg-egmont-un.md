# 04 — Basel Committee, Wolfsberg Group, Egmont Group and UN sanctions

Status: done (2026-10-06). Sections: 1 BCBS, 2a-2r Wolfsberg, 3 Egmont, 4 UN sanctions, 5 derived requirements
(B/W/E/U-xx), 6 sources. These bodies sit around FATF: the Basel Committee (BCBS) sets supervisory expectations for
banks, the Wolfsberg Group writes the industry's own standards (and the questionnaires correspondents send to Iraqi
banks), the Egmont Group connects FIUs, and the UN Security Council issues the sanctions lists banks must screen.

## 1. Basel Committee: "Sound management of risks related to money laundering and financing of terrorism"

Version read: BCBS guidelines, first issued January 2014, revised June 2017 (Annex 2 correspondent banking, Annex 4
account opening) and **July 2020** (supervisory cooperation, Annex 5). 62 pages, read from bis.org. The paper
supports FATF standards and links them to Basel Core Principle 29 ("abuse of financial services").

### 1a. Risk assessment and governance (paras 15-27)

- Comprehensive risk assessment of **inherent and residual** risk at country, sector, bank and relationship level;
  understanding built on the bank's **own operational and transaction data** plus external sources (NRA,
  international country reports); must be demonstrable to and acceptable to the supervisor (paras 15-16).
- **Board** approves and oversees risk and compliance policies and must understand ML/FT risk; risk information to
  the board must be "timely, complete, understandable and accurate" (para 17).
- Board and senior management appoint a qualified **chief AML/CFT officer** with stature and authority (para 18).
- **Three lines of defence** (paras 19-27):
  - 1st line: business units (front office, customer-facing) identify, assess and control the risks of their
    business; written procedures; internal procedures for detecting and reporting suspicious transactions; staff
    screening and **role-tailored training** (new staff trained as soon as possible after hiring; refreshers;
    scope and frequency tailored to the risk each employee faces).
  - 2nd line: the chief AML/CFT officer, compliance, **but also HR and technology**. The officer does ongoing
    monitoring of all AML duties, **sample testing** and **review of exception reports**, escalates to senior
    management or the board, and is the contact point for supervisors and the FIU. No business-line duties, no
    data-protection or internal-audit role (conflict of interest); direct reporting line to senior management or
    board; responsible for **reporting suspicious transactions**; sufficient resources.
  - 3rd line: internal audit reports to the audit committee and audits (i) adequacy of policies vs risks,
    (ii) staff effectiveness, (iii) **effectiveness of compliance oversight and quality control "including
    parameters of criteria for automatic alerts"**, (iv) training effectiveness; risk-based frequency; bank-wide
    AML audits periodically.
  - External auditors may evaluate AML controls; the bank oversees scope and expertise.

### 1b. "Adequate transaction monitoring system" (paras 28-31) — the most system-specific text in any standard

- A monitoring system **adequate to the bank's size, activities, complexity and risks**; for most banks,
  especially internationally active ones, effective monitoring "is likely to necessitate the **automation** of the
  monitoring process". A bank that decides it needs no IT system must **document** the decision and prove an
  effective alternative (para 28).
- When an IT system is used it **"should cover all accounts of the bank's customers and transactions for the benefit
  of, or by order of, those customers"** and enable **trend analysis** and identification of unusual relationships
  and transactions (para 28). (Coverage completeness is the classic enforcement failure; see file 20.)
- It should give senior management accurate information, including **changes in customers' transactional
  profiles**; customer profiles must incorporate updated, comprehensive CDD data (para 29).
- It should provide **centralised knowledge** organised by customer, product, across group entities and over time
  windows; without needing a single customer file, banks must be able to **risk-rate customers and manage alerts
  with all relevant information** (para 29).
- Parameters must be based on national and international experience of ML/FT methods; vendor **default parameters
  may be used but "must reflect and take into account the bank's own risk situation"** (para 29).
- The bank must be able to **set its own criteria** for additional monitoring, STR filing or other steps; the chief
  AML/CFT officer must have access to the system even if other business lines run it; alerts go to the officer for
  assessment; risk criteria must match the bank's risk assessment (para 30).
- **Internal audit evaluates the IT system** to ensure it is appropriate and used effectively (para 31).

### 1c. Customer acceptance, identification and risk profiling (paras 32-44)

- Acceptance policy identifies higher-risk customer types from the bank's risk assessment; factors: background,
  **occupation (incl. public or high-profile position)**, source of income and wealth, country of origin and of
  residence, products used, nature and purpose of accounts, **linked accounts**, business activities (para 32).
- Basic due diligence for all; simplified measures for proven lower risk; the policy must **not be so restrictive
  that it denies the general public access**, especially the financially or socially disadvantaged (para 33).
- EDD and senior approval for higher-risk customers (e.g. large balances with regular cross-border wires, PEPs);
  define when the bank refuses or terminates relationships (para 34).
- Identify and verify customers, persons acting on their behalf and BOs before transacting; best documents are those
  hardest to obtain illicitly or counterfeit; written BO declarations are allowed but **must not be relied on alone**;
  non-face-to-face is no excuse; ask why a customer opens an account far from the bank (paras 36-37).
- **Customer risk profiles** (para 38): purpose, level of assets, size of transactions, regularity, expected
  activity, transaction types, sources of funds/income/wealth; profiles per customer **or per customer category**;
  used to detect activity that deviates from "normal" for that customer or category; significant behaviour
  information must update the customer risk assessment.
- If CDD cannot be completed: do not open; if problems arise after opening, **close or block the account**; consider
  an STR; never inform the customer (para 41).
- Front office must be able to identify designated persons under UNSCRs (para 42).
- A customer refused by another bank over illicit-activity concerns: consider high risk, EDD, STR or refusal
  (para 43). Numbered accounts get full CDD; the AML officer and supervisor must have full access (para 44).

### 1d. Ongoing monitoring (paras 45-50)

- Understand the "normal and reasonable banking activity" of customers to identify **attempted and unusual
  transactions**; monitor **all** relationships and transactions, with intensity based on risk; enhanced monitoring
  for higher risk (para 45).
- Also carry out **"cross-sectional product/service monitoring"** to find emerging risk patterns (para 45), i.e.
  monitoring at product level, not only customer level.
- Scenarios should use the customer risk profile, CDD information and **information from law enforcement and other
  authorities** about schemes seen in the jurisdiction (para 46) — in Iraq, the AML/CFT Office scenarios.
- Using CDD data, detect transactions that make no economic sense, **large cash deposits**, or activity inconsistent
  with normal and expected transactions (para 47).
- Specific EDD policies for higher-risk customers: extent of CDD, **monitoring frequency, CDD refresh frequency**
  (para 48).
- **Integrated management information systems** give relationship managers and compliance/investigators timely
  information across lines of business: transaction history, **missing account-opening documentation**, significant
  changes in behaviour or business profile, unusual transactions (para 49).
- **Screen the customer database whenever sanction lists change**, and periodically to detect foreign PEPs and other
  higher-risk accounts (para 50).

### 1e. Information, reporting and freezing (paras 51-60)

- Record all CDD information: the documents **and** their transcription into the bank's IT systems (para 51).
- Retention at least five years from end of relationship; **records kept until any investigation or litigation
  closes** even if longer (para 52).
- Keep records of the **evaluation process** in ongoing monitoring and review and the conclusions drawn (para 53).
- Regular reviews to keep CDD accurate and up to date (para 54); demonstrate everything to the supervisor on request
  (para 55).
- Clear procedures for identifying, investigating and reporting suspicious transactions, communicated through
  training; reports must be relevant, timely and confidential; the chief officer makes **prompt disclosures where
  suspected proceeds remain in an account** (paras 56-57).
- After suspicion: review the customer's risk classification or the whole relationship, escalate to the right
  decision-maker, consider cooperation with law enforcement/FIU (para 58).
- TF funds may come from legal sources and be **very small amounts** (para 59); banks must identify and enforce
  freezing decisions (para 60).

What section 1 means for AKR-TMS:
- **Coverage reconciliation** as a core feature: prove that every account and every transaction type in the CBS is
  ingested and monitored (counts and sums reconciled daily against the CBS), because "cover all accounts ... and
  transactions" is the explicit expectation and coverage gaps are a top cause of enforcement (file 20).
- **Product-level ("cross-sectional") analytics** next to customer-level monitoring.
- Bank-specific parameters: ship with default scenario parameters (CBI pack) but force a documented bank-specific
  calibration before go-live, with the rationale stored.
- Separate roles that match the three lines: branch staff (1st line, observation log, liaison officers), compliance
  (2nd line, alerts/cases/STRs, exception reports, sample testing), internal audit (3rd line, read-only audit
  workspace including rule parameters and change history).
- Board-ready reporting: periodic AML risk report in "timely, complete, understandable and accurate" form.
- Missing-documentation and stale-CDD indicators visible inside the alert and case screens.
- Re-screen on every list change (same conclusion as file 03 F-13).

### 1f. Annex 2: correspondent banking, seen from the Iraqi respondent's side

Iraqi banks are mostly **respondents** to foreign correspondents. What correspondents are told to expect from them:
- **Disclose nested (downstream) relationships** accurately at onboarding and in periodic reviews (para 14); the
  correspondent assesses the number and type of nested FIs, their jurisdictions, services offered to them, and
  **the respondent's own due-diligence programme over its nested banks**, which must be updated periodically and
  provided on request (paras 15-16).
- **Respond promptly to requests for information (RFIs)** about transactions (para 17); RFIs are issued when
  unusual-activity alerts at the correspondent cannot be resolved (para 30).
- No expectation of KYCC: the correspondent does not do CDD on the respondent's customers (para 18); industry
  questionnaires (the Wolfsberg CBDDQ, section 2) are a starting point (para 19).
- **Full and accurate originator and beneficiary information in payment messages** so correspondents can screen and
  monitor (para 28); correspondents monitor for missing or **meaningless fields** and do **sample testing** of the
  respondent's payments (paras 34-35). The respondent, as ordering bank, must verify originator data (para 36).
- Before exiting, correspondents may limit services, apply **real-time monitoring**, sample testing or on-site visits
  (para 31); senior management is informed of high-risk relationships (para 32).

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- An **"outgoing payment quality" monitor**: before a correspondent finds meaningless or incomplete fields, the bank's
  own system should (R.16 rule pack, file 03 F-12).
- An **RFI desk**: log every correspondent RFI, link to the transactions and customers involved, track response
  time, assemble an evidence pack (KYC summary, BO, source of funds, prior alerts) in minutes. Response speed and
  quality are what keep USD relationships alive (file 01 section 4).
- A **nested-relationship register** for Iraqi banks that serve exchange companies or smaller banks, with their due
  diligence, and monitoring of flows from those institutions through the bank's correspondent accounts (the Al-Huda
  finding).

## 2. The Wolfsberg Group

The Wolfsberg Group is an association of 12 large global banks that writes non-binding but very influential
financial-crime standards. For Iraqi banks it matters twice: (1) their US/European correspondents run their
programmes on Wolfsberg standards, and (2) they must fill in Wolfsberg questionnaires (CBDDQ, FCCQ) to keep those
relationships.

### 2a. Statement on Effective Monitoring for Suspicious Activity — Part I (July 2024, 9 pages, read in full)

- Built on the 2019 Statement on Effectiveness and its **three "Wolfsberg Factors"**: (1) comply with AML/CTF laws;
  (2) **provide highly useful information to relevant government agencies in defined priority areas**; (3) establish
  a reasonable and risk-based set of controls.
- Uses "**Monitoring for Suspicious Activity (MSA)**" instead of transaction monitoring: customer behaviour and
  attributes combined with transactions; TM is a subset; MSA can also cover **employees, vendors and
  counterparties**.
- Diagnosis: the volume of SARs/STRs keeps rising but value does not; current programmes are built for technical
  compliance and keep ineffective habits:
  - expanding red-flag and typology coverage across all customers and products **even when the data show they
    produce almost no escalations**;
  - **"no SAR left behind"** tuning (every historical SAR must be re-detected), which causes over-alerting;
  - **defensive filing** whenever legitimacy cannot be fully verified.
  - Example: German FIU 2022, 337,186 STRs, only 15.3% forwarded to law enforcement, 95% of resulting cases closed
    without prosecution. Europol 2016: 2.2% of criminal proceeds frozen, 1.1% confiscated in the EU.
- Current metrics (typology coverage, alert volumes, alert productivity, **alert-to-SAR ratios**) measure quantity,
  not usefulness.
- Re-focus on: (1) **risk-based monitoring of material, observed ("crystallised") typologies**, using data on which
  routines produce valuable SARs to decide which to keep or stop (with regular testing of de-scoped routines);
  (2) targeted analytics and investigations aligned with **national priorities**; (3) holistic enterprise risk
  identification and **"stress tests"** for large hidden schemes (laundromats, mirror trades, mule networks).
- **Value of SARs**: best indicator is direct FIU/law-enforcement feedback; otherwise proxies (investigation
  complexity, **network activity found**, reporting in priority areas). New metrics: **false-negative assessment**
  (SARs that came from other sources such as front-line referrals but were missed by automated monitoring), and
  **completeness of SAR information**.
- Turn detective controls into **preventive** ones where high-value patterns are known (e.g. block transactions with
  shell companies in specific jurisdictions until reviewed).
- **Innovation**: legacy tools struggle with cross-border growth, volumes and faster payments; barriers include
  black-box solutions with low explainability, regulator upskilling, slow user adoption, cost. Innovation is a
  multi-year journey; beyond detection analytics, improve **case management, external data, control oversight and
  exploratory analytics**.
- **ML models** must be documented and analysed against crystallised outcomes; feed **case narratives and STR
  content back** into the platform (e.g. NLP extraction) to find emerging patterns and raise risk scores of
  connected entities.
- **Data**: modular, "Lego block" architecture (open-source components welcome); cloud helpful but **not essential**;
  inputs should include transactions, customer static data, internal lists, **dynamic behavioural data (device ID,
  IP address)**, and reputable external data (company structures, UBOs, watch lists); robust data governance and
  quality control.
- **Entity resolution and graph networks** are now central: link internal customer, account, transaction and
  external data into a network view; build risk profiles for customers, underlying customers, UBOs and other
  entities.
- **Dynamic segmentation**: static segments (customer type, industry, inherent risk) are outdated; combine known
  attributes with **statistical clustering (k-means, Jenks)** recomputed on an ongoing basis to cut false positives.
  Link MSA and periodic CDD (an MSA investigation can defer a periodic review; a recent review can de-prioritise an
  alert).
- **Visual analytics and self-service**: dashboards, visual link analysis, collaborative workflows; a sandbox to
  test new patterns and "what-if" scenarios; **below-the-line testing** and **champion-challenger** models; simply
  lowering thresholds by 10% is not meaningful false-negative testing.
- **Machine learning**: supervised and unsupervised methods are mature; **GenAI/LLMs are "in their infancy"** for
  this use and need research, validation and governance for bias and explainability; ML can be a **booster on top
  of rules** or the primary detector; also usable for **secondary name-screening to cut false positives** and to
  **auto-populate case files**. Train on many crystallised risks (manual cases, law-enforcement production orders),
  not only past automated SARs; balance recall and precision; "aiming for 100% recall ... is likely to lead to an
  ineffective system".
- **Enablers asked of regulators**: innovation statements, lighter model-risk overhead for financial-crime models,
  acceptance of missed SARs during innovation, sandboxes, **rejecting mandatory parallel runs** during transitions,
  information sharing; FIU feedback on individual SARs; public-private sharing of a **standard baseline feature
  set**.
- Glossary terms worth adopting in the product: above-/below-the-line testing, champion-challenger, crystallised
  risk, entity resolution, precision/recall, parallel run, sensitivity testing.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- Effectiveness metrics designed in: per-scenario productivity (alerts -> escalations -> STRs -> FIU feedback),
  false-negative analysis (STRs from branch referrals that no rule caught), STR completeness score.
- An **FIU feedback register** to capture any feedback from the Iraqi AML/CFT Office per STR (the FIU grades STR
  quality, file 01 section 5) and feed it back into rule tuning and model labels.
- Scenario lifecycle with retire/keep decisions backed by data, while keeping the CBI minimum scenarios always on
  (the CBI list is mandatory; Wolfsberg-style de-scoping applies only to the bank's extra scenarios).
- Entity resolution + network graph as a core view (customers, related parties, BOs, counterparties, shared phone,
  address, device, family number).
- Dynamic segments with clustering, plus a sandbox with below-the-line and champion-challenger testing.
- Preventive controls through the phase-2 pre-release API once a pattern is proven.
- Use LLMs only for assistive tasks with human review (case summaries, narrative drafting, Arabic adverse-media
  triage), not for decisions.

### 2b. Statement on Effective Monitoring for Suspicious Activity — Part II: Transitioning to Innovation (August 2025, 11 pages, read in full)

Part I is renamed "Moving Beyond Automated Transaction Monitoring". Part II gives a transition framework with three
pillars:

**A) Transition and validation**
- Start by admitting the legacy approach is near its limit; the new approach is different by design and need not
  re-detect everything the old one did ("drag net" scenarios produced low-quality, questionably suspicious filings).
- Automated monitoring is no longer the single "catch-all" pillar: analytics teams, a larger internal FIU and
  better-trained front office now produce more and better internal referrals.
- New success indicators: **priority risk coverage** (high-impact crime per the bank's threat assessment and
  national priorities); **expanded risk-indicator coverage** (non-transaction data); **precision rate**; **recall
  rate**; **internal and external feedback on SAR/STR quality** (including an internal re-grading of historical
  SARs to separate real suspicion from defensive filings, fed into training and acceptance criteria);
  **downstream integration** (the analyst can clarify activity with the customer through the relationship manager or
  contact centre).
- The classic **parallel run is "an artefact of a less advanced testing environment"**; validate instead with
  proof-of-concept testing on historical data against the new success criteria.
- Transition focus areas: explicit **product coverage** (what is in and out of scope); **investigator readiness**
  (ML alerts come from many factors, so use LLMs to surface the most predictive features and **generate
  investigator-ready summaries and next-best actions**); normal change governance (stable data, no big launches
  during heavy remediation).
- Validation is continuous; compare old and new outputs mainly to understand quality of SARs the new approach would
  miss and whether other controls cover them; SMEs rate samples of new alerts.

**B) Model risk balanced with financial crime risk**
- Prudential-style model risk management (MRM) is a major barrier; financial-crime models should not sit in the
  same risk tier as prudential models. Decide to run a model by weighing the risk of an imperfect model against the
  risk of **not** improving detection; "if an imperfect model identifies financial crime over and above what the
  institution would have detected without the model, it may still be appropriate to run the model".
- Materiality criteria to consider: detection effectiveness (not only SAR counts), product risk, **share of
  transactions covered**, **concentration** (sole reliance on one model), size of business covered.
- Non-redundant oversight across the three lines: MRM (2nd line) validates methodology, data quality, thresholds,
  back-testing, benchmarking; assurance (2nd line) checks control effectiveness against policy; audit (3rd line)
  checks governance, alert handling, escalation, typology gaps, overall effectiveness.
- Validate a **core model once**, then validate only the delta (e.g. data feeds) when rolling it out to other
  business lines or jurisdictions. Directly applicable to a multi-bank product: validate the AKR-TMS model family
  once, then per-bank data-feed and calibration validation.
- Cites the US agencies' April 2021 interagency statement on model risk management for BSA/AML systems, which
  supports fast adaptation of financial-crime models.

**C) Explainability** through three lenses:
- **Risk coverage**: rules map red flags to typologies to scenarios; for ML, map **typologies to data features** in
  a maintained inventory; ground features in the risk assessment, the NRA and FIU advisories; label model outputs to
  show which risks are captured.
- **Design and calibration**: the Group recommends a **hybrid of rules (well-defined risks and automatable
  reporting requirements), supervised models (where good investigation labels exist) and unsupervised models
  (unknown and emerging typologies)**; explain data selection, influential features, training set, choice of
  algorithm with strengths and weaknesses; re-train and re-calibrate because models deteriorate.
- **Usage**: users understand outputs alongside other controls; agree the risk appetite for missed activity (the ML
  equivalent of below-the-line testing); every alert must say **why** it fired (influential features, their risk,
  underlying indicators), ideally with **visualisation**; guardrails and pre-defined conditions documented and
  reviewed.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- Architecture choice confirmed: **CBI scenario rules + supervised scoring + unsupervised anomaly detection**, each
  alert showing reason codes and a feature-to-typology map.
- A **typology-feature coverage inventory** (CBI article -> typology -> rule/feature -> alerts) is both the
  explainability artefact and the coverage matrix requested by the CBI.
- LLM-generated **investigator summaries** in Arabic and English, clearly marked as drafts for human review.
- Product validation strategy for many banks: one core validation of each model version, then a per-bank delta
  validation pack (data mapping tests, calibration report).
- Customer-outreach integration: analysts can send a request for clarification through the branch/relationship
  manager (with tipping-off safeguards, file 03 F-16).

### 2c. Wolfsberg CBDDQ v1.4 and FCCQ v1.2 (2023): what Iraqi banks must answer to keep correspondents

Read in full (PDF from db.wolfsberg-group.org) together with the CBDDQ Guidance v2.0 (2023). These are still the
current versions as of October 2026 (the resources page lists no later CBDDQ/FCCQ). The CBDDQ is the questionnaire a
respondent bank (an Iraqi bank) fills in for every correspondent (US, European, Gulf banks clearing USD/EUR/AED);
the FCCQ is the short version for other relationship types. Answers are per legal entity, refreshed every 12-18
months (the declaration says "no less frequently than every eighteen months"), and signed by the Global Head of
Correspondent Banking AND the MLRO.

Structure of CBDDQ v1.4 (132 questions, 14 sections):
1. Entity and ownership (UBOs at 10%+, state ownership 25%+, bearer shares, offshore licence, virtual bank licence,
   LEI, regulator).
2. Products and services (downstream/nested relationships with domestic banks, foreign banks, MSBs/MVTSs/PSPs;
   processes to identify them; walk-in customers for wires, FX, cheque cashing and the due diligence level applied;
   VASPs; trade finance).
3. AML/CTF and sanctions programme (15 components: officer, adverse information screening, BO, cash reporting, CDD,
   EDD, independent testing, periodic review, policies, PEP screening, risk assessment, sanctions, SAR, training,
   transaction monitoring; staff count; board approval annually; board receives and challenges reporting;
   outsourcing; whistleblower policy).
4. Anti-bribery and corruption (EWRA in the last 12 months, residual risk rating, training by line of defence).
5. Policies and procedures (prohibit anonymous accounts, shell banks, unlicensed banks/remitters, Section 311
   entities; PEP risk assessment; exit and re-entry controls for customers exited for financial crime; internal
   watchlists; risk tolerance statement; record retention).
6. Risk assessment: separate AML/CTF EWRA and Sanctions EWRA, each covering inherent risk (client, product,
   channel, geography) and controls effectiveness (TM, CDD, PEP identification, transaction screening, adverse media
   screening, list management, training, governance, management information), completed in the last 12 months.
7. KYC/CDD/EDD (what is collected incl. expected activity, SoF, SoW; UBO identified and verified; minimum BO
   threshold; risk classification factors; site visits for high-risk entities; risk-based adverse media and PEP
   screening at onboarding, renewal and trigger events; method used; review frequencies by risk incl. "trigger-based
   or perpetual monitoring reviews"; **metrics on current and past periodic/trigger reviews**; 25 customer
   categories subject to EDD/restriction/prohibition: arms, respondent banks, embassies, extractives, gambling,
   general trading companies, MSB/MVTS, NGOs, non-residents, PEPs and close associates, precious metals and stones,
   charities, used car dealers, VASPs, travel companies and others; who approves EDD; QA on EDD files).
8. Monitoring and reporting (method: manual/automated/both; **name of the vendor/tool**; **when the tool was last
   updated**; **when the TM application was last calibrated**; STR procedures; escalation; **data quality
   management programme so complete data for all transactions is monitored**; timely response to RFIs from other
   banks; timely RFIs to own customers).
9. Payment transparency (adheres to Wolfsberg Payment Transparency Standards; complies with FATF R.16 and local
   rules; controls for accurate originator information and for beneficiary information incl. address and country).
10. Sanctions (policy approved by management; controls against using a correspondent's account to make it violate
    its own sanctions; controls against stripping, resubmission and masking; screen customers and their BOs at
    onboarding and regularly; method and vendor name; **when the matching configuration was last tested for
    effectiveness and completeness**; screen all sanctions-relevant data in cross-border transactions incl. free
    text; data quality for screening; lists used: UN, OFAC, OFSI, EU, other G7, other; **how many business days to
    load list updates into customer screening and into transaction screening**; physical presence in
    comprehensively sanctioned countries).
11. Training (content, audiences by line of defence, targeted training for high-risk activities).
12. Quality assurance and compliance testing (separate from audit).
13. Audit (frequency by internal and external audit; areas covered incl. **technology, transaction monitoring,
    name screening and list management, transaction screening, reporting/metrics and MI, SAR filing**; adverse
    findings tracked to completion).
14. Fraud (new in v1.4: fraud policy, dedicated team, **real-time fraud monitoring**, use of IP address, GPS
    location, device ID).

Changes in the 2023 iteration (v1.4/FCCQ v1.2): new Fraud section, whistleblower policy question, virtual bank
licence question, sanctions policy approval question, refresh cycle set to 12-18 months, completion guidance and
capacity-building guidance merged into one Guidance v2.0 document.

Points from the CBDDQ Guidance v2.0 that read like product requirements:
- Q84b (vendor or in-house tool): assess adaptability, update frequency and limitations; "the flexibility and
  scalability of a solution is of utmost importance"; keep an audit trail so the bank understands the tool and can
  show it to authorities.
- Q84b2 (last updated): regular review of parameters and criteria; reassess effectiveness to reflect business
  changes and new ML/TF methods (new scenarios); document every enhancement.
- Q84b3 (last calibrated): banks "cannot use an off-the-shelf calibration"; keep a methodology and an audit trail of
  tuning changes **approved by the MLRO**; tune regularly using historical alerts and SARs.
- Q86 (escalation): senior management visibility of **escalation volume and backlog**; MLRO informed of TM incidents.
- Q87 (data quality): periodic data integrity checks that data is completely and accurately captured in source
  systems and transmitted to the TM system; detect abnormally functioning rules (abnormal variation in transaction
  and alert volumes) caused by data problems.
- Q88/Q89 (RFIs): track response times both ways; delays in answering correspondents' RFIs damage the relationship;
  missing RFIs to customers lead to SARs that could have been explained.
- Q103: sanctioned references can appear in any field including free text; incomplete screening is a failure.
- Q107: a defined timeframe and process flow for loading updated lists across the organisation.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- **One-click CBDDQ evidence pack.** Many CBDDQ answers are facts AKR-TMS holds: tool name and version, date of
  last calibration with the MLRO-approved tuning log, date of last screening-effectiveness test, list-update lag in
  hours per list (customer and transaction screening), periodic review metrics by risk tier, EDD population by
  category, data-quality checks, RFI response times, STR process. Generate them as a dated, signed annex for the
  bank's CBDDQ (and for the US correspondents that de-risked Iraqi banks in 2023-2025, file 01).
- **List-update latency KPI** recorded automatically per list per update (UN, OFAC, EU, UK, IQTFS national list).
- **Screening tuning test harness** (seeded test names, Arabic transliteration variants) with a dated report:
  answers Q102a2 directly. See Wolfsberg sanctions screening guidance below.
- **Data-completeness monitor** per source table and per rule (volume anomalies, null rates, lagging feeds) to answer
  Q87/Q105, and the coverage reconciliation already planned from BCBS.
- **Nested/downstream relationship register** and walk-in customer controls (Q19), since walk-in wires and FX are
  common at Iraqi banks and exchange houses.
- **Exited-customer register** with re-entry detection (Q49k/l) and an internal watchlist module (Q49n).
- **Fraud signals**: device ID, IP and geolocation fields from digital channels can be mapped in (Q130), even though
  fraud is not the core scope; share the entity view with the fraud team.

### 2d. Wolfsberg Guidance on Sanctions Screening (2019)

Read in full. Still the Group's current screening guidance. Key content:
- Two controls: **customer/reference-data screening** (onboarding and lifecycle) and **transaction screening**
  (movements of value). Neither is enough alone; both sit inside a wider FCC programme.
- Screening is "the comparison of one string of text against another"; an alert is not by itself a sanctions risk.
  Hard parts: alphabets, languages, cultures, spelling, abbreviations, acronyms, aliases, fuzzy algorithms.
- Programme pillars: policies (what is screened, when, how alerts are adjudicated, how to decide when data is missing
  or unreliable); a responsible person; a risk assessment that decides **which attributes, when, which lists and how
  fuzzy**; internal controls with **documented configuration** that shows the setup can detect the bank's sanctions
  risks and is transparent about limitations; testing backed by metrics.
- Risk-based decisions must be documented: list subsets per jurisdiction, **exclusions**, **suppression / "good
  guys" lists**, removing data no longer risk-relevant. A governance file holds the rationale for every rule and
  threshold and for risk acceptance of deficiencies.
- Alert review: the tool must show all relevant bank data and list data side by side, support **maker-checker
  (four-eyes)**, and record the rationale for each decision.
- Metrics: alerts by list, by jurisdiction and by business; unintended data and list omissions; data integrity
  issues.
- Independent testing: confirm the tool generates the expected alerts and that thresholds and suppression rules
  behave within risk appetite; check accuracy and completeness of data uploaded. The screening tool may be treated
  as a model under model governance.
- Build-or-buy criteria: configurability of matching, screening rules to optimise alert creation and suppression,
  **support for non-Latin characters**, ad hoc/one-off manual screening, workflow configurability, metrics
  reporting, centralised or local installation, data integrity processes, integration.
- Reference data to screen: customers incl. **beneficial owners and connected parties**, **employees**,
  **third-party suppliers, landlords, tenants**, securities identifiers held in custody, recipients of donations and
  sponsorship. Screen at onboarding, then periodically, on trigger events and **whenever customer or list data
  change**; daily where data change often.
- Transaction screening scope: focus where detection is actionable before a violation; higher risk = cross-border,
  currency, routing. Real-time screening of cross-border payments is common practice; domestic payments between
  banks under the same rules may not need real-time screening (unless the bank is subject to another regime, e.g.
  the US dollar rules for Iraqi banks). Screen **before any commitment to move funds** and watch points where data
  could be changed or stripped.
- Attributes to screen: remitter and beneficiary, agents and intermediaries, banks and BICs, **vessels and IMO
  numbers**, **free text (SWIFT field 70)**, ISINs, trade documents (importer, exporter, manufacturer, notify party,
  shippers, freight forwarders, insurers, all banks in the chain), geography (fields 50/59, places of receipt and
  delivery, ports, countries of origin, destination and transhipment, airports). Amounts, dates and references are
  irrelevant to screening.
- OCR for trade documents needs QA validation.
- List management: list selection (regulatory, internal/private lists, geographic terms, BICs, prohibited securities
  and goods); impact of new lists on **alert spikes**; sourcing (vendor vs direct); maintenance with access control
  over who adds or removes entries; data enhancement (foreign-language variants, BICs); whitelisting of confirmed
  false positives; geographic scope; **exact vs fuzzy per list**; screening frequency and triggers. Watch the
  **delay between the regulator's update and the vendor's update**; new designations must reach screening "as quickly
  and accurately as possible".
- Internal ("private" or "grey") lists: minimum inclusion criteria and data quality, set time frames, regular review
  so intelligence does not go stale.
- Identifying information (DOB, nationality, place of birth) helps adjudicate but need not be screened. **Weak
  aliases** should not be screened: short strings, aliases with digits, nickname words (the example given is an
  Arabic-style nickname "Ahmed the Tall"), geographic references, common prefix plus one name.
- **Lookbacks**: when a miss is found (e.g. a name variation), fix the configuration or list and decide whether a
  historical review is warranted (root cause, repeat likelihood, public-policy or law-enforcement interest,
  enforcement mitigation, conduct issues).

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- Screening configuration is a governed, versioned object: per-list exact/fuzzy setting, thresholds, exclusions,
  good-guy list, each with a rationale, approver and date (maker-checker), exportable as the "documented
  configuration" examiners and correspondents ask for.
- Screen more than customers: BOs, authorised signatories, guarantors, **employees** (KYE), suppliers, landlords and
  tenants from the bank's procurement data, and counterparties in transfers, LCs and guarantees.
- **Weak-alias handling** and **Arabic name intelligence** matter more for Iraq than anywhere: the IQTFS national list
  is Arabic-only with mother's name and birth year (file 01); patronymic chains (name, father, grandfather, tribe)
  and nickname forms ("Abu X") need dedicated logic (file 22).
- Alert screen shows the customer record next to the list record with distinguishing attributes (DOB, mother's name,
  nationality, place of birth) highlighted, and a one-click rationale picker plus free text.
- Every list update is diffed; new entries trigger an immediate **delta re-screen of the whole customer base and
  recent counterparties**; the list-update lag is recorded per list (CBDDQ Q107).
- **Lookback tool**: re-run a corrected configuration or a new alias against N months of history from the read-only
  CBS replica and produce a report; this suits the read-only architecture well because all history is available.
- Metrics dashboard: alerts by list, branch, product and channel; spike detection after list loads; data omissions
  (customers with missing DOB, nationality, ID number).

### 2e. Wolfsberg Principles for Using AI and ML in Financial Crime Compliance (December 2022)

Read in full (short paper). The Group supports AI/ML in FCC programmes provided data-ethics principles are applied,
outcomes are "fair, effective, and explainable", and performance is monitored for stability after go-live. Five
principles:
1. **Legitimate purpose**: guard against misuse or misrepresentation of data and against bias; integrate ethical and
   operational risk assessment into risk governance; **data collected for FCC must not be reused for other purposes
   without separate review**.
2. **Proportionate use**: weigh benefits against risks; assess the severity of the financial-crime risk against the
   model's **margin for error**; validate use and configuration regularly.
3. **Design and technical expertise**: control and understand the technology, its limits and consequences; diverse,
   skilled teams able to spot bias; design starts from clearly defined intended outcomes; results must be
   "adequately explained or proven given the data inputs"; senior stakeholders must understand the tools well enough
   to decide when and how to use them; ongoing testing, validation and re-configuration.
4. **Accountability and oversight**: the bank is responsible for AI/ML decisions **whether built in-house or bought
   from a vendor**; train staff; oversight by people responsible for ethical data use; processes to challenge the
   technical teams.
5. **Openness and transparency**: be open about AI/ML use within legal limits, but without revealing detection
   logic that helps evasion or breaching STR confidentiality and data protection; engage regulators; educate
   customers.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- Because the bank stays accountable for a vendor's model, AKR-TMS must hand each bank the material to own the
  decision: model cards (purpose, data, features, known limits, margin of error), validation reports, drift
  dashboards, and a plain-language summary for senior management and the board.
- Purpose limitation built in: AKR-TMS data and model outputs are tagged for AML/CFT use only; any export for other
  uses (marketing, credit) is blocked by default and requires an approved exception.
- Bias checks: report alert rates and outcomes by segment (governorate, branch, gender where recorded, nationality,
  age band) so unfair concentration can be seen and challenged; this also protects against "de-risking" of whole
  groups.
- Detection logic disclosure controls: the full scenario thresholds are visible only to authorised compliance and
  audit roles; branch users see reason codes but not exact thresholds (prevents insiders from structuring around
  them).

### 2f. Wolfsberg Payment Transparency Standards (October 2023) and Roles and Responsibilities (2024)

Read in full. The 2023 Standards replace the 2017 version and use ISO 20022 vocabulary: debtor (originator), debtor
agent PSP (ordering bank), intermediary agent PSP, creditor agent PSP (beneficiary bank), creditor (beneficiary),
ultimate debtor/creditor. "PSP" covers banks and non-banks alike: "a payment is a payment".

Scope: cross-border payments; domestic payments as local rules require (incl. domestic payment market
infrastructures, PMIs); all fiat currencies incl. CBDCs; **all values**; card transactions whose intent is a
person-to-person transfer.

Standards for all PSPs:
- Never omit, delete or alter debtor or creditor information to avoid detection (anti-stripping); changes only to
  correct errors or add detail.
- Reflect every party and agent in the right fields so the next PSP can monitor and screen them.
- Cooperate with other PSPs' requests for information within the law.
- When combining domestic rails with cross-border transfers, choose the domestic PMI that keeps the cross-border
  nature and party data visible.
- Move to structured formats (ISO 20022) as early as possible.

Debtor agent (an Iraqi bank sending a payment):
- CDD on customers and on walk-in/occasional users; R.16-compliant messages; decide from the payment data whether
  the payment is suspicious or breaches sanctions; keep records to reconstruct messages.
- Determine and flag whether the intent is domestic or cross-border.
- Use LEI or BIC where available; correct message type; ISO 20022 category purpose codes; correct application of
  bundling rules ("one-to-many" like salaries, "many-to-one" like merchant collections, and high-risk
  "many-to-many" like bundled remittances); card "goods and services" exemptions; collection-agent models.
- **Originator data**: name as verified in CDD (registered legal name for companies, not trade names); address as
  verified, sufficient for screening, structured or at least hybrid (structured town and country), avoid P.O. boxes,
  ISO 3166 two-letter country codes acceptable; account number, or a unique transaction reference when there is no
  account. Policy must say which name and which address are used (joint accounts, multiple addresses).
- **Beneficiary data**: name, address (strongly encouraged, "best practice", fewer RFIs) and account number, passed on
  as supplied by the customer, not verified by the sender.
- **On-behalf-of (OBO) payments** (corporate payment factories, law firms paying for clients): enough CDD to confirm
  OBO payments fit the customer's business; policy on what ultimate-debtor data customers must provide; put the
  ultimate debtor's name and address in the message; a payment on behalf of another PSP is a correspondent
  relationship, not OBO; watch for entities acting as unlicensed PSPs.

Intermediary agent (e.g. the Iraqi bank's USD correspondent, or an Iraqi bank acting as correspondent for exchange
companies and smaller banks):
- Own laws, PMI rulebook and R.16; risk-based policy to **execute, reject or suspend**; full CDD on respondent PSPs;
  confirm the respondent's payment model works as agreed (bundling, collection agent, goods and services); pass all
  data on unchanged; monitor the respondent's flows; keep records.
- Not responsible for CDD on parties it has no relationship with, nor for unbundling bundled payments.

Creditor agent (an Iraqi bank receiving a payment):
- Own laws; execute/reject/suspend policy; full CDD on the beneficiary; instruct customers on correct use of virtual
  account numbers; decide from the message whether incoming payments are suspicious or breach sanctions.

Roles and Responsibilities (2024) adds a matrix per actor. Debtor and creditor agents and every intermediary must
"monitor for **manifestly meaningless or incomplete information** in payment messages", request more information
and/or reject payments with insufficient information, and the creditor agent raises RFIs when data is deficient.
Monitoring can be "real-time or ex-post monitoring as may be required by applicable laws". Intermediaries in a
cover-payment chain see only what each previous PSP passed on.

What it means for AKR-TMS (adds to file 03 F-12, the R.16 pack):
- **Payment-quality monitor** on outgoing and incoming transfers: missing or meaningless originator/beneficiary
  fields ("NA", "XXX", "customer", repeated characters, the bank's own name as originator), missing address or
  country, P.O.-box-only addresses, trade name instead of registered name, missing account or UTR, missing purpose.
  Results per branch, per correspondent and per counterparty bank, with repeat-offender tracking. Ex-post works
  over the read-only link; real-time hold needs the phase-2 API.
- **Name consistency check**: the originator name in the outgoing message must equal the CDD-verified name in the
  CBS customer table; flag differences (stripping or substitution risk).
- **Bundling and OBO detection**: flag accounts that send or receive many third-party payments (law firms, general
  trading companies, exchange houses, payment processors) without ultimate-party data; fits CBI scenarios on
  accounts used as pass-through.
- **Domestic-leg-of-cross-border detection**: domestic transfers that fund or follow a SWIFT/international leg within
  a short window, a common way to hide the cross-border nature.
- **RFI register** for incoming and outgoing RFIs about payments, with time-to-answer KPIs (CBDDQ Q88/Q89).
- ISO 20022 readiness: the canonical transfer model in AKR-TMS stores structured party data (name, structured
  address, country, DOB, LEI/BIC, purpose code) even when the CBS holds only MT-style free text, and parses free
  text into structure where possible.

### 2g. Wolfsberg/ICC/BAFT Trade Finance Principles (2019 amendment)

Read the core paper and Appendix I (documentary credits). Joint with the ICC and BAFT, written so that small local
banks (not only global banks) follow it; "do not require a bank to have significant electronic systems".
- Scope: documentary credits (LCs, UCP 600), documentary collections (URC 522), demand guarantees and standby LCs,
  open-account payables finance and receivables discounting, bank-to-bank trade loans. Open-account trade (most of
  world trade) gives the bank only the clean payment, so only standard AML and sanctions screening applies.
- TBML techniques: **over-invoicing, under-invoicing, multiple invoicing, short shipping, over shipping, deliberate
  obfuscation of the type of goods, phantom shipping**; usually collusion between buyer and seller, often controlled by
  the same persons.
- Banks "deal with documents, not goods" and are generally **not equipped to judge unit prices**; but "manifestly
  unusual" unit prices should prompt enquiries. Dual-use goods are hard to spot; staff need awareness and escalation.
- Regulators increasingly expect banks to compare onboarding knowledge (counterparties, countries, goods, expected
  annual volumes) with the actual trade transactions: **an active exchange between CDD information and transaction
  information**.
- Two kinds of risk information: data that must be validated **before** a transaction proceeds (sanctioned name,
  sanctioned country, vessel) and data used in **post-event** analysis and STR work.
- DC risk indicators (pre and post): deal beyond the customer's capacity or substance; improbable goods, origins,
  quantities or destinations; unusual complexity; import/export controls possibly breached; blatant value-versus-
  quantity anomalies; goods out of line with the customer's business; sanctioned names or countries; attempts to
  disguise the countries involved; illogical or last-minute payment instructions; **third parties funding the DC**
  ("just in time" credits to the settlement account); DCs **constantly amended or extended** or **routinely cancelled
  or unused**; connected applicant and beneficiary; applicant controls the documents; goods descriptions differ
  between invoice and transport documents; unexplained third parties; advance waivers of discrepancies; missing
  transport documents; significantly overdrawn DC.
- Screening points across the DC lifecycle: issuing request (parties, goods, ports, DC structure, risk indicators),
  advising, presentation of documents, payment. Vessel and voyage checks through third-party databases or the
  IMB, by risk.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- A **trade finance module** reading LC, collection and guarantee tables from the CBS (most Iraqi CBSs have a trade
  module; CBI scenarios include 9 LC and 4 LG scenarios, file 27): rules for amendments and extensions, cancellations,
  unused LCs, third-party funding of margins, connected applicant/beneficiary, goods outside the customer's declared
  business, high-risk ports and transhipment countries, repeated beneficiaries across unrelated applicants.
- **Declared-vs-actual trade profile**: compare the customer's KYC trade profile (goods, counterparties, countries,
  annual volume) with LCs, collections and import-related transfers.
- **Unit-price outlier check** only where the data exists (goods code, quantity, unit), framed as "manifestly unusual",
  not as a pricing judgement.
- Pre-event items (names, countries, vessels) go to screening; post-event items feed TM and case files. Trade
  documents arrive as scans, so screening of document data is manual or OCR-assisted with QA (out of scope for phase
  1 beyond the structured LC fields).
- Iraq relevance: import LCs for the government and private sector are large and were central to the dollar-auction
  abuse (file 01, 02); the CBI's cross-check of import documents and customs data is a strong source for TBML signals.

### 2h. Wolfsberg Source of Wealth and Source of Funds FAQs (2020)

Read in full. Written for private banking but applied widely (PEPs, high-risk customers, EDD).
- **SoW**: the economic activities that generated the customer's overall net worth (family wealth, inheritance,
  gifts, settlements, pensions, lottery/casino wins, property and asset sales; salaries, bonuses, business ownership
  and profits; investment income). The aim is not to verify the exact net worth but to judge whether the
  wealth-generating activities are **legitimate and plausible**; include an **estimate of net worth** and at least the
  main contributors.
- **SoF**: the origin and means of transfer of the funds deposited: amount and type, method of transfer, remitting
  party and its bank, country of origin. Third-party funding with no apparent link to the customer requires
  establishing and recording the relationship.
- Collect at onboarding, refresh by risk; "not a documentary exercise"; significant gaps or material inconsistencies
  need clarification, documents or independent enquiries.
- **Corroboration** from independent reputable sources (company registries, land registry, banks, accountants,
  lawyers, notaries). Worked example: a property sale funds the account; the land registry proves ownership and price
  appreciation; the incoming wire from the lawyer's account confirms the SoF.
- SoF corroboration triggers: high-risk customer; **material physical cash**; funds from gambling; inconsistency with
  the customer's background.
- If evidence falls short: deny onboarding, raise the risk rating, restrict products, heighten monitoring, or exit.
- Retail customers who look like HNWIs need segmentation rules to catch them outside the private bank.
- Risk assessment factors: net worth documented; initial and ongoing SoF documented; corroborated where required;
  unexplained or incoherent SoW/SoF; adverse media; reputation; SoW/SoF from high-risk industry or country; PEP
  status.
- Exceptions (old customers without documents, local customs) must be documented with mitigating controls and
  governance.
- Ongoing: update SoW on periodic or event-driven reviews, change of residence, risk increase, or when activity shows
  SoW has changed materially; monitor **actual vs expected SoF**; consider prior TM inquiries and SAR history.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- An **SoW/SoF record** per customer (structured categories, estimated net worth, expected annual inflows by source,
  evidence attachments in the evidence vault, corroboration status), mandatory for EDD customers and PEPs.
- **Actual-vs-declared monitoring**: compare inflows (cash, transfers, counterparties, countries) against the
  declared SoF and income; mismatch is both a risk-rating factor and an alert reason. This is the same principle as the
  CBI's KYC-declared-vs-actual controls (file 01).
- **Third-party funding detector**: payments into an account from parties with no recorded relationship, especially
  for loans, LC margins and investment accounts.
- **Cash-intensity factor**: material cash deposits trigger SoF evidence requests; matches Iraq's cash economy and the
  CBI cash scenarios (file 27).
- Iraqi corroboration sources to support in the evidence vault: real estate registry (Tabu) documents, company
  registry extracts (Ministry of Trade registrar), salary letters from ministries (large public-sector payroll),
  inheritance court documents (sharia court certificates).

### 2i. Wolfsberg FAQs on Negative News Screening (2022)

Read in full. Negative news (adverse media) = "information available in the public domain which FIs would consider
relevant to the management of Financial Crime risk" (online news, court records, regulatory disclosures, penalty
notices). FATF mentions adverse media searches as an EDD measure. Not zero-tolerance and not needed everywhere.
- Value: reveals criminal involvement (may trigger lookbacks of past transactions); informs risk rating and monitoring
  intensity; acts as a **CDD trigger event**; adds context to investigations; tests the SoW/SoF story.
- Risk-based scope: broad structured and unstructured searches for HNWIs, PEPs, large corporates and FIs; targeted
  structured searches or none for mass retail.
- Configure by **risk category** (ML, TF, corruption, organised crime, drugs, human trafficking, wildlife, PF, tax
  evasion) and **risk stage** (allegation, investigation, charge, conviction): earlier stages give more alerts.
  Exclude non-financial-crime matters (traffic fines, public order).
- Reputational risk is assessed separately from financial crime risk: allegations disproved in EDD may still leave
  reputational risk for the risk owner to decide.
- Scope: customers, UBOs, related parties; optionally vendors and suppliers. Batch, manual or real-time.
- Decisions to document: type (manual, search engine, vendor, internal), scope, frequency, sources, categories and
  stages, **look-back period** (multi-year; after the first review only new events), alert filtering (geography, **DOB
  tolerance +/- 1 year**, delta screening, auto-discounting), data purge, operating model, QA, MI.
- **Multi-script**: transliteration, transcription and translation differ (the paper's translation example is Arabic:
  the Arabic name of Abu Dhabi). Screen in the local script where sources exist; otherwise transliterate customer
  names to Latin. Local teams may need to source local-language media and document why sources are included or
  rejected.
- Source credibility: international agencies and national papers with editorial oversight score higher; be careful
  with social media, editable content, opinion pieces; consider **press freedom** (Reporters Without Borders index)
  and polarised or state-controlled media; corroboration across sources; editorial coherence.
- Disinformation is a real risk; mitigate through source evaluation.
- Vendor evaluation: coverage in the bank's markets, data completeness and timeliness, matching quality and false
  positives, archive of removed articles, translation, configurability, dashboards, **traceability of every
  screening event and configuration**, data-sharing limits.
- Search engines are not databases, results vary by user and time, content can be purged; use consistent settings,
  agreed key-word strings in the right language; precise offence words beat generic stage words.
- Alert handling: tiered (L1 discounting by rules, L2 specialists); rules-based (e.g. close when DOB differs by more
  than a year) and risk-based judgement; document auto-discounting rules and review their performance; false-positive
  discounting by name difference, secondary identifiers (DOB, incorporation date, gender) and weaker situational data.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- An **adverse media module** that works with Arabic sources: Iraqi and regional Arabic media (and Kurdish), Iraqi
  court and Integrity Commission (Nazaha) announcements, CBI and FIU circulars, Ministry of Interior wanted lists,
  plus international feeds through a licensed vendor where the bank pays for one.
- Configurable categories and stages; Iraq-specific emphasis on **corruption and embezzlement of public funds**,
  smuggling (oil, fuel, goods), drugs (Captagon/crystal meth transit), terrorism, human trafficking (file 02 threat
  ranking).
- Store every article snapshot in the evidence vault (articles get taken down), with source credibility rating
  and a disinformation flag; record the analyst's materiality decision and reputational-risk note separately.
- Media hit becomes a trigger event: raises the risk score, opens an event-driven review and can start a lookback of
  the customer's history over the read-only CBS replica.

### 2j. Wolfsberg Guidance on the Risk-Based Approach (June 2026) and Statement on the RBA (2025)

Read in full. Replaces the 2006 RBA guidance; aligned with the FATF's 2025 R.1 changes. Three elements a bank must
demonstrate: **proportionality, prioritisation, effectiveness**.
- **Proportionality**: programme sized to the business model (size, scale, footprint, customers, risk appetite). Use
  the national risk assessment and public-sector dialogue. A **risk appetite** set by senior management says which
  risks are accepted and which are not tolerated, and must take **financial inclusion** into account (alternative
  measures for customers who would otherwise be excluded). Business-wide risk assessment across customers,
  transactions, geographies, products, channels; targeted assessments (customer, industry, country, product) giving
  low/medium/high ratings that drive CDD level, approvals, review frequency, governance, escalation and monitoring.
  Risk assessment must be **dynamic and embedded in business as usual** through MI and control-effectiveness
  reporting, not a once-a-year document. Avoid duplicated, inconsistent or siloed assessments.
- **Prioritisation**: "A focus on everything is a focus on nothing." Spend on higher-risk customers and activities and
  **stop, reduce or redesign** controls that are redundant, duplicative or unproductive.
  - Who the customer is: non-residents, nominee shareholders, bearer shares; cash-intensive businesses, MSBs,
    third-party payment processors; complex structures without commercial rationale; **newly formed entities**;
    listed companies (lower risk, lighter controls); exposure to higher-risk countries (e.g. FATF-listed).
  - What the customer does: behaviour judged against stated purpose, expected activity and profile; **repeated or
    unusual amendments to key profile data without a business reason**; evasive conduct, delays or contradictory
    answers to outreach; activity misaligned with turnover, wealth or business model; corridors that do not match
    the profile; product use that increases anonymity, complexity or reduces traceability; insights from screening and
    monitoring fed back into prioritisation.
- **Effectiveness**: the Wolfsberg Factors (comply with law; reasonable risk-based controls; highly useful information
  to authorities in priority areas), Demonstrating Effectiveness (2021) and Auditing for Effectiveness (2024).
  **Traceable governance** for trust with supervisors and auditors, without needless complexity.
- Risk-based supervision: supervisors and auditors should move beyond tick-box checks, "zero-failure" expectations and
  "no SAR left behind", and test outcomes tailored to each bank's profile.
- Training: role-specific for high-risk roles, critical thinking, speak-up culture, tone from the top.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- **Dynamic risk assessment**: the enterprise-wide risk assessment (EWRA) is fed live from AKR-TMS data (customer
  mix, product and channel volumes, country exposure, alert and STR outcomes, control test results) rather than a
  yearly spreadsheet; the CBI requires a documented RBA reviewed at least every two years (file 01), and this keeps
  it current between reviews.
- **Risk appetite register**: tolerated and prohibited customer types, countries, products, with thresholds that the
  risk-rating and onboarding checks reference; breaches show on the board pack.
- **Profile-amendment monitoring**: frequent changes to address, phone, occupation, income, authorised signatories or
  beneficial owners in the CBS customer tables (visible through the read-only link's change history or snapshots)
  are a risk signal and a classic insider-collusion indicator.
- **Control rationalisation report**: per rule and per screening configuration, show productivity and overlap so the
  bank can justify switching off unproductive scenarios (with CBI approval where the scenario is mandated).
- **Financial inclusion lane**: simplified-due-diligence segment for low-risk accounts (CBI SDD threshold below IQD 1
  million, file 01) with compensating monitoring, so inclusion products (e.g. payroll localisation accounts) are not
  over-alerted.

### 2k. Wolfsberg Statement on Countering Terrorist Financing (2024)

Read in full. Replaces the 2002 statement. Key points:
- TF funds need not be criminal in origin (legitimate business revenue, donations to exploited charities) and the
  use of funds need not link to a specific attack to trigger reporting.
- Banks must prevent terrorists accessing services, screen and report, respond promptly to authorities, while
  balancing **access to finance** for communities (avoid blanket de-risking).
- Controls: risk-based EDD for sectors, activities and jurisdictions linked to TF (informed by competent
  authorities); understand the bank's geographic footprint; screen customers and BOs at onboarding and ongoing;
  **report positive matches against terrorist lists**; trigger-event reviews incl. negative news; records retrievable
  quickly; screen names and transactions; monitor against the expected profile; give "highly useful information" in
  priority areas.
- Asks of authorities: coordinated official lists with enough identifiers for efficient searching, **feedback on TF
  STRs**, actionable indicators and typologies, legal protection for information sharing within groups and between
  banks.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- A **TF typology pack** tuned to Iraq: ISIL remnants and financing through extortion, kidnapping and cash couriers;
  hawala and exchange-company corridors to Syria and Turkey; NPO and charity accounts (FATF R.8); small, frequent
  transfers from many senders to one beneficiary in high-risk governorates (Nineveh, Anbar, Salah al-Din, Kirkuk,
  Diyala) and border areas; **camp-related flows** (Al-Hol repatriation context); top-ups and card withdrawals near
  conflict zones. Details go in file 27 Part B and file 02.
- Terrorist-list matches skip the normal queue: immediate hold where the phase-2 API exists, immediate MLRO
  notification, freeze workflow and the report to the CBI/IQTFS committee and FIU within the legal time.
- NPO segment with its own risk factors (registration with the NGO directorate, foreign funding, cash withdrawals
  near conflict zones).

### 2l. Wolfsberg Country Risk FAQs (2024)

Read in full. Defines **Financial Crime Country Risk (FCCR)**: the residual level of financial crime in a country
after considering inherent risk and the effectiveness of its AML/CTF framework. An elevated rating does not forbid
business; it asks whether controls are strong enough.
- Inputs: criminal indicators (corruption and terrorism indices, drugs, tax havens incl. the EU non-cooperative tax
  list, human trafficking, environmental crime); political factors (stability, regulatory effectiveness, rule of law,
  press freedom, civil liberties); regulatory factors (home/host regulator lists, FATF/FSRB MERs); national risk
  assessments; optional economic indicators. Choose reputable sources, check data quality, avoid double counting,
  make the effect of each source on the score traceable. Also **nexus risk** (proximity, trade or cultural links),
  especially for sanctions and TF, but bordering a sanctioned country is not automatically a nexus.
- Review at least **annually**, plus ad hoc on triggers: FATF statements and grey/black list changes, new MERs, new
  sanctions or embargoes, coups and unrest, new local high-risk lists, new NRA. Grey/black listing should push a
  country to the higher/highest rating; delisting with evidence of sustained effectiveness can lower it. Decide
  whether a rating change triggers a **retrospective review** of exposure or only applies going forward.
- Sanctioned regimes default to the highest rating; consider comprehensive vs sectoral sanctions (sectoral risks are
  often better handled by industry-specific controls than by country rating). SDN-type listings have no geography.
- Methods: statistical combination, **weighted average** of normalised inputs, or zero-base additive; output as a
  0-10 score or categories (RAG; low/medium/high/higher). SMEs review outputs. Vendor indices may be used as an input
  or a benchmark, but the bank must understand and be able to share the vendor's methodology with supervisors.
- Scope: all ISO 3166 countries; sub-national ratings for free-trade zones and offshore centres; overseas territories
  rated separately when their AML regime differs from the parent's.
- Model or methodology is a governance decision; either way needs independent review, change control, KPIs (score
  sensitivity, volatility, benchmark against vendor ratings), and coverage re-validation.
- Missing data: unknown is itself a risk; do not let defaults make naturally high-risk countries look low; consider a
  documented subjective override.
- **Overrides**: rare, justified, approved at a senior and independent level, with an **expiry date** and at least
  annual review; recurring overrides mean the methodology is broken.
- Ownership: an independent FCC function owns it; ratings are pushed to CDD, TM thresholds and scenarios, EWRA, and
  reference-data teams within a set timescale, with records of the date each system applied each change.
- Country exposure points for customer risk: residence/incorporation; country of origin of SoW and SoF; countries in
  transactions; industry-country combinations; PEP country of exposure; booking entity country.
- Uses: portfolio analysis (share of customers and assets from high-risk countries), CDD/EDD choice and approvals, TM
  thresholds and frequency, risk appetite, review cycles.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- A **country risk table** shipped with a documented default methodology (weighted inputs: FATF status, Basel AML
  Index, Transparency International CPI, UN/US/EU sanctions programmes, CBI high-risk country circulars, press
  freedom), versioned, with per-bank overrides that need a rationale, approver and expiry date.
- **Iraq-specific inputs**: CBI and AMLCFT Office lists of high-risk countries (file 01), the US dollar-clearing
  restrictions; nexus flags for Iran, Syria, Turkey border regions and Lebanon corridors; sub-national flags where
  relevant (e.g. free zones; Kurdistan Region as same country but different supervisory practice for some sectors).
- Trigger-driven update: a FATF plenary outcome (February, June, October) or a new sanctions programme updates the
  table, then AKR-TMS recalculates affected customer risk scores and lists customers and transactions exposed, with an
  option for a retrospective lookback.
- Country risk flows into the customer risk score, the TM thresholds and the periodic-review cycle, with an
  "effective from" date recorded per change for audit.

### 2m. Wolfsberg Principles for Auditing for Effectiveness (2024)

Read in full. Gives internal audit (3rd line) a framework built on the Wolfsberg Factors (order changed for audit):
1. **Comply with financial crime laws and regulations.** Principle 1: the bank shows its governance documents address
   every local legal requirement and that controls ensure adherence. Measures: 1.1 law-to-policy mapping (where group
   policy is stricter, use it unless it conflicts; track local deviations); 1.2 controls mapped to those policy
   elements are designed and operating effectively; 1.3 a governed, periodic and trigger-based refresh of the mapping
   when regulations change, with gap remediation.
2. **Reasonable, risk-based set of controls.** Principle 2: controls proportionate to the risk assessment and
   effective. Measures: 2.1 coverage proportionate to assessed risks; 2.2 effectiveness; 2.3 governed change process
   for adding, stopping, reducing or redesigning controls; audit validates changes and points out controls that no
   longer produce outcomes.
3. **Highly useful information to government agencies.** Principle 3: optional indicators on usefulness of STRs and
   public-private partnership contributions. Measures: 3.1 credible indicators; 3.2 evidence they are collected; 3.3
   board-level oversight with examples of how reports helped law enforcement. Effectiveness may also show as the
   **absence** of downstream interdictions or asset blocks.
- Audit runs at least annually by risk, tracks findings to remediation and validates fixes, including regulator- and
  self-identified issues.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- **Regulatory obligations register** (CBI law 39/2015, CBI instructions 2023, 2025 controls articles, FIU guidance,
  FATF R.x), each obligation linked to the policy clause, the AKR-TMS control (rule, screening config, workflow
  step, report) and its test evidence. This is the "law-to-control map" audit asks for (Measure 1.1/1.2) and the same
  structure as the CBI coverage matrix (file 01).
- **Control change log** with risk rationale and approvals; productivity data to justify stopping or redesigning
  controls (Measure 2.3).
- **STR usefulness indicators**: FIU feedback (accepted, graded, referred to law enforcement, asset freeze), quality
  scores, time to file, share of STRs in national priority areas (corruption, TF, drugs); board-ready examples.
- **Audit workspace**: read-only role with full history, sample selection tools and exportable evidence for internal
  audit, external auditors and the CBI-approved assurance firm.

### 2n. Wolfsberg Guidance on PEPs (2017)

Read in full. Still the Group's PEP guidance.
- Focus on **grand corruption** (Transparency International definition). Most PEPs do not abuse their position.
- Wolfsberg favours a risk-based approach for **all** PEPs, foreign and domestic, while the FATF says foreign PEPs are
  always high risk; local rules prevail (Iraq follows FATF: foreign PEPs high risk, domestic PEPs risk-based, file
  01 and 03).
- Factors that make a public function PEP-level: country's corruption vulnerability, official responsibilities,
  honorary vs salaried, authority over government activity and officials, access to state assets and funds, power
  over tenders and contracts, links to corruption-prone industries.
- Typical PEP functions: heads of state and government, ministers; senior judges whose decisions are not appealable;
  senior military officers; ruling royals; senior executives of important state-owned enterprises; senior officials
  of major political parties. Possibly: heads of supranational bodies; MPs; ambassadors; central bank board members;
  mayors and governors of regions.
- **Close family**: spouse (or equivalent), children and their spouses, parents, siblings. Ethno-cultural or
  religious links may require extending the definition. **Close associates**: widely and publicly known close
  business colleagues or personal advisers, especially in a fiduciary financial role. Relatives and associates are
  not automatically PEPs themselves.
- Identification: ask at onboarding; screen customers and key principals against a PEP database (internal or
  vendor); reputable public information; staff training. Close family and associates are harder to identify.
- Biggest risk: a PEP opening an account **outside the country of political exposure** with no rationale.
- PEP-controlled entities: an entity created solely for the PEP (personal investment vehicle, trust) is treated like
  the PEP; listed companies, regulated FIs and well-governed SOEs/central banks with a PEP on the board carry far
  less risk. SOEs carry real PEP risk where rulers appoint relatives or state and personal finances are not
  separated.
- Framework: identify new and existing customers (incl. **dormant accounts reactivated**); risk assess; EDD (time in
  office, title, country; purpose; SoF and SoW, validated independently when risk is high; negative news; where a
  relative is funded by the PEP, document the PEP's SoF/SoW); **senior management approval**; enhanced monitoring;
  periodic review with re-approval on material change (death, divorce); **portfolio-level PEP exposure review against
  risk appetite**; training.
- **Declassification**: against "once a PEP, always a PEP" except for some higher-risk PEPs; consider country
  corruption risk, post held, time in office and chance of return, SoW transparency, industry links, plausibility,
  adverse media, continuing political connections. Senior management approves and documents; keep the prior PEP
  flag for investigations.
- Screening at onboarding, periodic review and trigger events; screen account holders, BOs (settlors, beneficiaries),
  and controllers. Minimum PEP data: all names and aliases, DOB or year of birth, country of exposure, gender, role(s)
  with appointment dates, date left office, deceased flag. Close-match (not exact) screening with tolerance for
  transpositions, variants and typos; auto-discounting only on reliable clear differences (e.g. birth year).
  **Delta screening** both ways (new or changed PEP records against all customers; new or changed customers against
  all PEPs); or a "no-match" list keyed on the **customer-PEP pair**, invalidated when either record changes
  materially.
- When screening finds a PEP the front office missed, find out why and fix procedures or training.
- Governments should publish lists of PEP positions per country.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- **Iraqi PEP register**: positions list by tier (Presidency, Council of Ministers, ministers and deputies, Council of
  Representatives members, Federal Supreme Court and Court of Cassation judges, governors and provincial council
  members, KRG president, prime minister, ministers and parliament, heads of independent commissions incl. Integrity,
  Elections, CBI governor and board, senior army and police commanders, PMF leadership, heads of major SOEs such as
  SOMO, North/South/Basra Oil companies, Rafidain/Rasheed banks, political party leaders), plus holders and dates; the
  CBI's 26 August 2026 PEP circular and PEP Controls No. 2 of 2023 set the local definition (file 01).
- Family model fits Iraq's **patronymic and tribal naming**: relatives often share father's and grandfather's names
  and the tribe/laqab; use these for candidate relative detection with human confirmation, never auto-classify.
- Pair-keyed false-positive memory and bidirectional delta screening for PEP and sanctions lists alike.
- PEP lifecycle states: active, former (with left-office date), declassification proposed, declassified (flag
  retained), deceased; senior-management approval steps and re-approval triggers.
- Dormant-account reactivation triggers PEP and sanctions re-screening (CBI also freezes dormant accounts after six
  months, file 01).
- PEP portfolio dashboard for the board: number, tier, products, balances, flows, EDD status, overdue reviews.

### 2o. Wolfsberg Financial Crime Principles for Correspondent Banking (2022) and FAQs

Read in full. Replaces the 2014 principles. Iraqi banks sit on both sides: respondents of US/EU/Gulf
correspondents, and correspondents for smaller Iraqi banks, exchange companies and MSBs.
- Correspondent banking = services by one bank to another for third-party payments, trade and paper clearing in a
  currency; ongoing and repetitive. Higher risk when the respondent serves its own customers through the
  relationship. May be extended by risk to NBFIs, PSPs, MSBs/MVTS, fintechs, VASPs.
- Governance: named responsible personnel; a correspondent-banking governance body (1st and 2nd line);
  board-approved **risk appetite** listing prohibited or limited parties and activity through respondents' accounts,
  communicated to respondents.
- Due diligence factors: respondent's jurisdiction (FATF statements, sub-national risk), group relationships
  (own branches and affiliates are respondents too), ownership and management (state/public/private, UBOs, PEPs in
  management or ownership, recent ownership changes), products offered (cross-border payments, non-customer
  services, physical cash, low-transparency products), customer base, services used, licensing and regulatory history,
  FCC programme quality, **no shell banks**, site visits. Collect via the CBDDQ. Periodic refresh plus trigger events
  (adverse media, unusual activity escalations).
- EDD: PEP involvement; **downstream (nested) FIs**: types, scale, locations, their customers, and whether the
  respondent checks their controls and payment transparency; higher approval levels.
- Monitoring: use CDD results and risk rating in monitoring; feed monitoring results back into periodic review (the
  **feedback loop**).
- Ongoing review uses: STRs on the respondent and its customers, adverse media, activity inconsistent with expected
  activity, unexplained volume/value changes, **non-compliance with payment transparency**, sanctions metrics,
  **non-response or poor response to RFIs**, and the gap between what the respondent said about its programme and what
  is observed.
- Avoid: shell banks (and banks dealing with them), unlicensed or unregulated banks/NBFIs, unresolved significant
  concerns, respondents unwilling to remediate. Payable-through accounts are high risk.
- Expectations of respondents: partnership, timely and full RFI answers, respect for the correspondent's risk
  appetite and restrictions.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- **Correspondent and nested-relationship module** for Iraqi banks acting as correspondents: respondent profiles
  (licence, owners, PEPs, products, customer base, CBDDQ on file, site visit dates), expected activity, risk rating,
  review cycle; monitoring of respondent flows against expected activity; payment-transparency quality per
  respondent; RFI response tracking; prohibited-activity checks from the bank's risk appetite.
- **Respondent self-view for Iraqi banks as respondents**: the same metrics the correspondent will compute about the
  Iraqi bank (payment data quality, RFI response times, sanctions hits, unusual volume changes) shown to the Iraqi
  bank first, so it can fix issues before its USD correspondent de-risks it. This is a strong selling point given the
  2023-2025 US dollar restrictions on Iraqi banks (file 01).
- Exchange companies and MSBs that bank with Iraqi banks are effectively downstream relationships: treat them as a
  segment with their own scenarios (pass-through flows, unusual corridors, cash intensity).

### 2p. Wolfsberg Transaction Monitoring RFI Best Practice Guidance (August 2022)

Read in full (30 pages: 10 pages of principles plus an appendix of 30 common RFI questions, each with "why is this
asked" and "what is the expected response"). Source: https://wolfsberg-group.org/resources/general/57 (asset PDF
"Wolfsberg Group RFI Best Practice Guidance", downloaded as wb-rfi-2022). For Iraqi banks this is the most
practical Wolfsberg paper: it is the script their USD correspondents follow when a payment from an Iraqi customer
trips the correspondent's monitoring.

Scope and purpose
- Covers RFIs raised by **transaction monitoring** in correspondent banking (and other payments-based relationships).
  It explicitly excludes RFIs about payments blocked or rejected by sanctions screening or payment-transparency
  events (those follow the Payment Transparency Standards, section 2f).
- Ineffective RFIs or incomplete answers cause cost, follow-up rounds, **defensive SAR/STR filings by the
  correspondent**, relationship reviews and **restrictions in payment processing**.
- Why correspondents send RFIs: the activity of concern is usually by the respondent's customers, whose KYC the
  correspondent cannot see. Typical asks: KYC/background of the underlying customer, summary of the account
  relationship, purpose of the transaction(s), nature of the pattern, details of the counterparty.
- An RFI also "evidences the Respondent's ability to manage risk and provide comfort around its controls" — every
  answer is graded as evidence of the respondent bank's control quality, not just about the one customer.
- **The respondent should treat an incoming RFI as an input to its own transaction monitoring or as a manual trigger
  for a local investigation** if it had not already looked at the activity.
- Drivers: TM AML/CTF concerns, FIU review projects, internal escalations/unusual activity referrals, account activity
  reviews, other financial crime risk factors.

Roles and responsibilities
- Correspondent (issuer) should: (1) avoid unnecessary RFIs by first checking internal records, public domain and prior
  RFIs; (2) send RFIs only to its own customer (the respondent), about the respondent's underlying customer or the
  underlying customer of a downstream bank; (3) ask only what is pertinent, **with a brief rationale**; (4) write
  clear, succinct questions that allow for linguistic and cultural differences; (5) use established points of contact
  and **state the expected timeline**; (6) tell respondents its risk appetite and information-sharing expectations
  before the relationship starts; (7) encourage collaborative discussion; (8) run internal feedback loops so
  coverage teams know about concerning answers or non-responses.
- No expectation that the correspondent does due diligence on the respondent's customers ("KYCC" — FATF correspondent
  banking guidance, October 2016).
- Respondent should: (1) set clear points of contact and assign each RFI to an accountable person promptly; warn of
  delays early; (2) check what its own systems hold and, if not enough, **contact the customer**; for a downstream
  bank's customer, ask the downstream bank; (3) gather the information in a reasonable period and **update CDD
  records** if needed; (4) answer every question in a structured, easy-to-understand way so no follow-up is needed;
  (5) where possible and lawful (confidentiality, banking secrecy, **tipping-off rules**) say if the subject is or
  was already under internal investigation or has been exited; (6) have customer terms and conditions that permit
  sharing information with correspondent banks.

Timelines
- Indicative response window **10 to 30 business days**, depending on the correspondent, complexity, age of the
  transactions and regulation. The correspondent should state the deadline when issuing, leaving itself time to
  conclude its investigation.
- Respondents should start processing **without delay**, especially when customer outreach is needed (customers need
  time to reply), and ask for more time early when records are old or a downstream bank must be asked.
- The correspondent's evaluation of the answer "is likely to form part of the Correspondent's overall assessment of
  the risk of the relationship".

Insufficient or missing responses
- Disposed of risk-based; in many jurisdictions missing information leads the correspondent to **file an STR**.
- Respondents that consistently answer late "should recognise that this may trigger additional concerns" — poor
  answers can indicate the respondent cannot manage its risks.
- Correspondents should keep **metrics, governance and an internal escalation process** on RFI timeliness and
  quality, review trends in governance forums, and may reassess the relationship, risk profile and control
  environment.
- Measures against the RFI subject: notices to the respondent to **restrict payments from/to the subject** until a
  sufficient answer arrives.
- A written agreement before onboarding can fix expectations on comprehensiveness and timeliness and on how the
  correspondent will monitor answers to judge the respondent's CDD and AML/CTF controls.
- Respondents in consent-based jurisdictions should have customer T&Cs that allow disclosure to correspondents.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- **Incoming RFI workspace (Iraqi bank as respondent).** Log each RFI from a correspondent (sender, reference,
  received date, deadline, subject customers, transactions, questions). AKR-TMS has almost every answer in the
  read-only CBS replica and its own records, so it can **pre-fill a draft response** per question: account opening
  date, account types and purpose, expected vs actual activity, residency, status and closure reason, KYC profile,
  SoW/SoF record, UBOs and signatories, counterparty history and whether the counterparty also banks with us, rating
  history with reasons, last periodic review date, screening and adverse-media history, linked documents. The
  compliance officer edits, adds the rationale (Q22) and approves (maker-checker) before it is sent. LLM help is only
  for drafting, with human approval (section 2b/2e principles).
- **Deadline clock and outreach tracking**: business-day clock against the stated deadline (default 10 business days
  if none stated), reminders, logged extension requests, customer outreach tasks with their own due dates (and
  downstream-bank requests for exchange companies and smaller banks).
- **Every incoming RFI opens or updates an internal case** about the subject customer (the guidance says to treat it
  as a manual trigger); if no alert ever fired on that activity, it is logged as a **"missed by our monitoring"**
  signal that feeds scenario tuning and the below-the-line review (sections 2a/2b).
- **Tipping-off guard**: fields that would reveal an internal investigation, an STR or an exit are flagged and need
  MLRO approval before they appear in any outgoing text (Iraqi Law 39/2015 art. 12/Fourth and Instructions art. 15,
  see file 01). The customer outreach task template is worded neutrally.
- **Q17 patterns become our own scenarios**, so the Iraqi bank sees them before its correspondent does: many-to-one and
  one-to-many on the same or consecutive days, repeat payments to the same party, round amounts, round trips, multiple
  same-day transfers to one party, sequential cheques to one payee, personal accounts used for business payments
  (Q16), and flows shaped around FX or transfer restrictions (Q18, highly relevant to Iraq's dollar controls).
- **RFI KPIs for the respondent self-view and the CBDDQ evidence pack** (section 2c, Q88/Q89): RFIs received per
  month and per correspondent, median and 90th-percentile time to answer, share answered by the deadline, share
  needing follow-up rounds, top question types, RFIs about customers we had already alerted on vs not.
- **Outgoing RFI workflow (Iraqi bank as correspondent for exchange companies, MSBs and smaller banks)**: a pre-RFI
  checklist (internal records, public sources, prior RFIs), question templates with rationale per question taken
  from the 30-question list, deadline, response quality grading, escalation of non-responses to a governance view,
  and an option to **restrict payments for the RFI subject** until answered. Non-response trends feed the respondent's
  risk rating (section 2o) and can prompt an STR decision.
- Covers sanctions and payment-transparency RFIs too, with a category field, although Wolfsberg treats those under
  the PTS (section 2f).

### 2q. Wolfsberg Guidance on Digital Customer Lifecycle Risk Management (2022)

Read in full (9 pages). Source: https://wolfsberg-group.org/resources/innovation/56 (downloaded as wb-digital-2022).
Builds on the FATF Guidance on Digital Identity (2020). Onboarding itself stays in the CBS for AKR-TMS, but the
paper's core idea — **trigger-based refresh instead of calendar refresh** — sits squarely inside AKR-TMS's KYC scope
(risk rating, periodic reviews, CDD/EDD).

Main points
- With the right controls, non-face-to-face engagement can be a **standard or even lower-risk channel** (FATF Digital
  Identity 2020) and an enabler of financial inclusion. The face-to-face meeting no longer gives a material advantage
  in knowing the customer.
- Three developed controls: (1) wider identification and verification with more emphasis on **authentication**
  (proving the person using the account is the person onboarded); (2) a **dynamic, holistic customer risk profile**;
  (3) **trigger-based ongoing due diligence** that refreshes data when something changes, putting resources on
  priority risks in real time instead of fixed review cycles.
- Wider identity attributes: beyond legally required data (e.g. date of birth), use email, phone number, device
  identifiers, location data (with consent), behavioural biometrics. The customer builds a **"progressive identity"**
  over time. Systems must be structured to update and track these variables.
- High-quality identity data: APIs to government or third-party registries (corporate registries, voter rolls,
  professional registers) verify self-reported data; LEIs for legal entities; services confirm document, phone and
  email authenticity and compare device location with declared residence. Multiple verification paths are legitimate,
  in line with risk appetite, regulation and **local financial-inclusion strategies** (older phones must not bar
  people from authentication: combine ownership, knowledge and inherent factors).
- Dynamic risk assessment: the classic factor groups remain (customer, geography, products/services/transactions,
  delivery channel), but in digital relationships the channel becomes the ordering principle. Two variables matter
  most: the **strength of the initial authentication event** at onboarding (and binding of credentials to the
  device) and the **level of information access** the customer grants (IP, GPS; a VPN or blocked location is
  legitimate but reduces what the bank can see, so it may require more customer contact).
- "Follow the data": map confirmed identity-theft and onboarding-fraud cases back to the onboarding method to learn
  which authentication paths are weak.
- Trigger-based refresh method: **break each risk factor into observable variables, map each variable to an internal
  or external data source, set thresholds that signal a possible change, and define a targeted action proportional
  to confidence** (e.g. device behaviour change: a low-level re-authentication; long stay abroad seen in IP data plus
  a government registry confirming non-residence: automatic profile update). Thresholds start theoretical or
  vendor-supplied and are recalibrated from outcomes.
- The chain (factor, variable, threshold, action) must be **explicit, regularly calibrated and assessed for
  trustworthiness**, with documented assumptions: a registry API is only as good as its data; re-authentication is only
  as strong as the original binding; location monitoring depends on the access the customer grants.
- High-confidence triggers can refresh data invisibly; lower confidence needs customer contact.
- Data ethics: design expertise, openness with customers and regulators about data use, accountability; responsible
  technology that is fit for purpose, secure, privacy-preserving, consent-based and accessible.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- **KYC refresh trigger engine.** Each trigger is a governed, versioned object: risk factor, observable variable,
  data source (a mapped CBS table/column, a transaction feature, a screening or media event, an external list),
  threshold, action (auto-update, targeted data request, re-rating, full EDD review), owner and calibration date.
  Triggers from data AKR-TMS already reads: ID document expiry, change of address/phone/employer/signatories in the CBS
  (profile-amendment monitoring, section 2j), declared vs actual activity breaches, turnover crossing the simplified-
  due-diligence ceiling (IQD 1 million tier, file 01), new product or channel taken, dormant account reactivation,
  first transfers to a new high-risk country, minors reaching adulthood, company registration or licence expiry, UBO
  changes, PEP/sanctions/adverse-media hits, and one phone number, email or device shared by unrelated customers.
- **Targeted refresh, not a full re-KYC**: the task sent to the branch or relationship manager names the exact data
  element to refresh and why; the result is recorded against the trigger. Periodic reviews by risk level stay (Iraqi
  rules still require them), but triggers pull the next review forward and periodic reviews of quiet, low-risk,
  recently-refreshed customers can be lighter.
- **Trigger effectiveness metrics**: share of triggers that confirmed a real change, time to refresh, refresh backlog
  by branch; thresholds recalibrated through the same governed change log as scenarios (section 2m).
- **Onboarding assurance level as a profile attribute**: read the onboarding channel from the CBS (branch with
  original ID, agent, digital eKYC with or without liveness) and store it as a risk input; report identity-fraud and
  mule cases by onboarding channel ("follow the data").
- **Optional read-only link to the bank's digital channel database** (mobile and internet banking, e.g. a platform
  like digital-banking-system) for login country/IP, device ID, new-device binding, and channel events. This enables:
  residence deviation (long periods of foreign IP use by a customer declared resident in Iraq), account takeover and
  mule indicators (many customers on one device, new device right before a large transfer), and better geography
  inputs. The same read-only rule applies: no calls into the channel's backend.
- **Financial inclusion lane**: low-risk, low-value customers onboarded through simplified paths are monitored by
  triggers and automatic upgrades to full CDD when they cross thresholds, rather than being excluded (supports the
  CBI SDD tier and Wolfsberg RBA 2026, section 2j).
- Privacy: device and location data are used only for AML/CTF and fraud purposes stated to the customer, with
  retention limits (purpose limitation, section 2e).

### 2r. Wolfsberg Guidance on the Provision of Banking Services to non-bank PSPs (2026)

Read in full (13 pages plus an appendix of 5 flow diagrams). Source:
https://wolfsberg-group.org/resources/correspondent-banking/206 (downloaded as wb-psp-2026). Builds on the
correspondent banking principles (2o), the Payment Transparency Standards 2023 and Roles 2024 (2f), and the **June
2025 revision of FATF R.16** (file 03). Directly relevant: Iraqi banks hold the accounts of Iraq's **17 e-payment
companies** (whose clients' transactions run through bank accounts) and of money transfer companies (file 01); the
NRA rates exchange companies "high" and e-payment companies "moderate" (file 02).

Scope
- Non-bank PSP = any provider that moves funds without a banking licence: settlement of payments for goods and
  services, domestic and cross-border remittances, card, e-money, mobile and other digital transfers. Includes MSBs,
  third-party payment processors, fintechs, e-money institutions; useful also for AISPs/PISPs.
- Excluded: pure physical currency exchange or cheque cashing (so Iraqi class C currency-exchange shops fall
  outside, but transfer-performing exchange companies are in), pure technology vendors that never touch funds,
  licensed banks (use the correspondent principles), firms paying only their own operating expenses, and pure VASPs
  (separate Wolfsberg digital-asset papers 2024/2025).
- Principles: risk-based approach; **payment transparency**, with the debtor-agent PSP primarily responsible for
  complete and accurate messages and intermediaries passing data unaltered and monitoring for gaps; and a thorough
  understanding of the PSP's business model, customers and controls.

Business models and flows
- Three activity types: **business remittance services** (payments, cash management, FX for corporates, SMEs and other
  FIs, including other PSPs); **person-to-person remittances** (harder to validate than business payments;
  distinguish digital-only providers from **cash-focused money transmitters**; includes gaming and social platforms
  where tokens can be cashed out); **merchant acquiring** (collecting card or e-wallet payments and settling to
  merchants through collection and settlement accounts).
- Related products: cash-management/FX platforms, wallets or stored-value accounts, cards issued to businesses for
  payments to suppliers or staff (cards used by third parties).
- Bank partnership models: **Banking-as-a-Service** (the PSP onboards customers and records KYC and transaction data on
  its own platform; the bank relies on it for records, so third-party risk management matters) and **sponsored
  accounts / sponsored FBO** (the PSP uses the bank's licence to access payment systems; the bank stays ultimately
  responsible for what passes through its accounts).
- Flow types: **proprietary** (own salaries, principal-to-principal; lower risk, but watch PSPs labelling customer
  pre-funding as "liquidity management", which hides third-party exposure), **third-party** (bank exposed to the PSP's
  customer base, like correspondent activity; may need a safeguarding account), and **bundled / netted / bulk** flows:
  one-to-many (e.g. salaries) lower risk; **many-to-one (merchant servicing) and many-to-many (remittances bundled then
  unbundled at destination) higher risk**, with many-to-many cutting transparency the most.
- Key risks: payment transparency (the bank sees only one leg of a multi-rail chain, e.g. card collections in one
  country bundled into an international wire and paid out domestically elsewhere), sanctions (blind spots in bundled
  chains; reliance on the PSP's screening), ML (no view of originators, purposes and final beneficiaries), and fraud
  (PSPs lack long account histories, so fraud is caught late).

Risk-based due diligence
- Where a PSP does several activities, apply the **most stringent** standard. Use **tailored questionnaires** (business
  model, flows, corridors, controls). Ongoing due diligence matters more than usual because PSP models change fast;
  **trigger reviews** on material negative news, ownership changes, financial crime incidents, licence changes, or new
  flows or intermediaries.
- Business model factors: service types; cross-border share (higher risk); **high-risk funding methods** (cash, money
  orders, prepaid cards); non-resident customers, especially in high-risk jurisdictions; **partner networks** for
  disbursement or collection (identify nesting and who does which AML/sanctions control); **agents** (oversight,
  licensing, who performs KYC).
- Licensing: document licence status and check it matches the activities the bank supports.
- Jurisdictions: the PSP's operating jurisdiction and its parent's; **payment corridors**; the PSP's customers'
  locations (e.g. merchant countries); nested entities' jurisdictions and supervision quality.
- Customer-type risk appetite: the PSP's appetite must align with the bank's; resolve gaps by agreeing which flows the
  bank will support.
- Controls to assess at the PSP: compliance staff (MLRO, CCO) qualifications and numbers; governance and scalability;
  CDD (UBOs, risk ratings, periodic reviews, EDD triggers); third-party due diligence (agents, distributors, partners)
  overlaid with anti-bribery controls; product risk assessments, caps and limits; **IP geolocation monitoring and
  blocking of sanctioned jurisdictions**; name screening (lists, frequency, connected parties, adverse media); payment
  screening (real time or not, vendor, fuzzy logic, **free-text fields**, lists); suspicious activity monitoring
  (manual or automated, alert volumes, **backlogs**, real time or post-event, scenarios, escalation); **use of AI/ML and
  its independent assessment**; management information (backlogs in TM and KYC renewal, open audit points, root
  causes, remediation plans); R.16 / travel rule compliance; fraud monitoring (IP/device tracking, fake documents,
  response speed, fraud metrics); SAR process and metrics; audit scope, frequency and findings; anti-bribery; training;
  enterprise risk assessment.
- Additional factors: operating history and past enforcement (new PSPs get more frequent reviews during growth);
  growth strategy (new products and markets vs ability to scale controls).
- **Account activity reviews**, distinct from suspicious-activity monitoring: look for shifts in payment volumes, **new
  payment corridors**, patterns of possible sanctions breaches, emergence of high-risk underlying customers or
  jurisdictions, **unexpected nesting or falling payment transparency**; results feed the periodic EDD review.
- Outcome: onboard/maintain or decline/exit by risk appetite and by the specific flows supported; where reduced
  end-to-end visibility cannot be mitigated, use a risk-control strategy (e.g. restricting flows).

Appendix flow examples (useful for the plan's diagrams)
- 1.1 simple P2P: payers pay the PSP's account at Bank 1; Bank 1 sends a **bundled** transfer to Bank 2 in country B;
  Bank 2 pays beneficiaries at Bank 3.
- 1.2 nested remittance: a local PSP (or small bank) aggregates remittances and sends a bundled payment plus per-
  beneficiary instructions to a global PSP, which moves a bundled payment cross-border and disburses; nested for the
  global PSP's bank.
- 1.3 and 1.4 merchant acquiring: card issuers net-settle to the card scheme's bank, which pays the acquirer's bank in a
  bundle, which pays merchants; in 1.4 a second PSP disburses to merchants the first PSP never onboarded (nesting).
- 1.5 e-wallet provider: wallets loaded by cash at agent shops, bank transfers, cards and other wallets; outflows to
  merchants' bank accounts ("cash out" hides the underlying sales), remittances, wallet-to-wallet (domestic or abroad),
  and ATM or cardless QR cash withdrawals. The bank holding the pooled customer account sees only the pooled flows
  unless the PSP shares its ledger.

What it means for AKR-TMS:
- **PSP and MSB customer segment** with its own risk model and scenarios, separate from ordinary corporates: segment
  flag on the customer (e-payment company, money transfer company, exchange company with transfers, payment
  aggregator, BaaS partner), licence record with expiry (CBI licence category), flow-type classification
  (proprietary / third-party / bundled), declared corridors and expected volumes.
- **Account activity review** dashboard per PSP, as Wolfsberg describes it (not alert-driven): volume trends, new
  corridors and counterparties, share of cash funding, share of bundled payments, payment-data completeness of the
  PSP's messages, unexpected new nested parties, sanctions hits. Changes beyond thresholds open an EDD review task
  (trigger engine from 2q).
- **Scenarios for PSP accounts**: "liquidity management" transfers that behave like customer pre-funding; many-to-one
  and many-to-many bundled flows; settlement-account outflows to parties that are not merchants; sudden corridor
  shifts; cash-intensive loading through agents; flows from or to unlicensed transfer businesses; round-tripping
  between a PSP's accounts at different banks (where visible); inflows from other PSPs' pooled accounts.
- **PSP due-diligence questionnaire** template in the KYC module covering the controls list above (MLRO, screening tool,
  free-text screening, TM backlog, AI use, R.16 compliance, IP blocking, agent oversight), stored in the evidence vault
  with review dates; the "most stringent activity" rule drives the PSP's rating.
- **Optional ingestion of the PSP's underlying transaction file** (per-beneficiary instructions or wallet ledger
  extracts the PSP shares with the bank, for example as a periodic file) so bundled payments can be unbundled and
  screened; without it, the system marks such flows as **"opaque"** and reports the opaque share to the MLRO.
- **R.16 2025 implications**: when the bank is the intermediary or creditor agent for PSP payments, the payment-quality
  monitor (2f) checks originator/beneficiary completeness on PSP-originated messages and tracks it per PSP.
- In Iraq this also covers the bank's own role in **CBI e-payment and mobile-wallet** ecosystems (wallet top-ups and
  withdrawals at agents, card-linked wallets): the CBI AML/CFT controls for e-payment service providers (20 April 2025,
  file 01) set what the PSP itself must do; AKR-TMS covers the bank's oversight of those relationships.

## 3. The Egmont Group of Financial Intelligence Units

### 3a. Iraq's membership (from the MER of May 2024, read earlier as iraq-mer-2024-en)

- Iraq's FIU, the **Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Office** (the "Office", under the CBI),
  **joined the Egmont Group in July 2023** (MER para 420, c.29.8 "Met"; R.29 rated Largely Compliant — the remaining gap
  was a possibly insecure channel for sending analysis results to the Public Prosecution presidency).
- Before membership, counterpart FIUs **refused MoUs or any exchange outside the Egmont network** (MER para 823); 7
  outgoing requests were refused for that reason alone (para 840), and the US FIU required Egmont membership before
  exchanging information (para 839). In 2018-2022 the Office exchanged requests by a dedicated encrypted e-mail
  address (para 824).
- Since accession the Office uses the **Egmont Secure Web (ESW)**; it received 20 cooperation requests through it in
  the first months (para 823). Outgoing requests in the assessment period: 382 (plus 6 spontaneous), 286 final answers,
  27 initial answers, 62 still pending; responses took 3 days to a month (para 840).
- As a member the Office must apply the Egmont principles: prior consent of the providing FIU before onward use
  (Law 39/2015 art. 29/Second), confidentiality per Egmont procedures, and conducting inquiries on behalf of foreign
  FIUs (MER paras 534-536).
- Egmont membership is also cited among Iraq's channels of international cooperation alongside the Global Coalition
  against ISIL, UNITAD and the relationship with OFAC (para 68).

### 3b. Egmont Principles for Information Exchange between FIUs (revised July 2025)

Read in full (15 pages). Source:
https://egmontgroup.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/EG-Principles-for-Information-Exchange-Revised-July-2025.pdf
(first approved July 2013; amended May 2022, January 2023, and July 2025 at the Luxembourg plenary). The Principles and
the Egmont Charter are **binding**; significant non-compliance goes to the Egmont Support and Compliance Process.
Information exchange between FIUs runs over the **Egmont Secure Web** (operated by FinCEN for the Group) or an
equivalent network such as FIU.NET.

Points that reach banks (the rest governs FIU-to-FIU conduct)
- Principle 14: besides what reporting entities file, **the FIU must be able to obtain and use additional information
  from reporting entities** as needed for its analysis. Principle 18: an FIU must be able to run **domestic queries on
  behalf of a foreign FIU** and pass on whatever it could obtain domestically. So a request from a foreign FIU
  becomes a request from the Iraqi Office to the Iraqi bank, and the bank's answer may travel abroad.
- Principle 19 (new 2025): FIUs must be able to exchange quickly the information needed for **provisional measures,
  confiscation (FATF R.4) and to suspend or withhold consent to a transaction**. A bank may therefore receive an urgent
  suspension or freeze instruction that originates in another country.
- Principles 20-25: requests must be specific and proportionate, explain the case and the link to the country, justify
  long time periods, and mark and justify urgency (urgent only when a timely answer is critical).
- Principle 28: FIUs should acknowledge receipt and give at least an **interim, partial or negative response within 30
  business days**; for urgent requests, within the time stated. The Office can only meet this if banks answer it fast.
- Principle 30: secrecy or confidentiality laws binding financial institutions are **not** a valid reason to refuse
  cooperation (legal privilege aside); nor is a fiscal angle or an ongoing domestic inquiry (unless assistance would
  impede it).
- Principles 23 and 26: feedback on the use and outcome of information exchanged; spontaneous disclosures are
  encouraged and the receiving FIU should report back when they lead to a successful investigation.
- Principles 34-41: exchanged information is used only for the stated purpose; onward dissemination needs the sending
  FIU's prior consent (consent is assumed for authorities named in the request unless refused); encryption,
  authentication, secure decommissioning and access limits; information from foreign FIUs must be stored so other
  domestic authorities can reach it only within the consent given.
- Principle 46 (new 2025): FIUs should have an **emergency response framework** for time-sensitive threats.

### 3c. Egmont Operational Guidance for FIU Activities and the Exchange of Information (revised May 2024)

Read the bank-relevant parts (receipt, analysis, power to obtain information, Annex C on STR quality). Source:
https://egmontgroup.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/EGMONT-Group-Operational-Guidance-for-FIU-Activities-and-the-Exchange-of-Information-%E2%80%93-Revised-May-2024.pdf
Unlike the Principles, this guidance is **not binding** (not subject to the Support and Compliance Process).

- **Receipt function**: besides STRs, FIUs may receive threshold-based reports: cross-border cash and bearer instruments,
  cash transactions, foreign currency transactions, wire transfers (domestic or international), complex unusual large
  transactions, accounts held abroad, and **reports on the application of targeted financial sanctions**. Iraq: CTRs
  at USD 10,000 and STRs, both through goAML; the Office also uses goAML for correspondence with reporting entities
  (MER para 278, file 01).
- **Feedback** to reporting entities on the usefulness of their reports and on outcomes, including trends and patterns,
  mindful of judicial secrecy and the presumption of innocence.
- **Analysis**: FIUs grade cases high (full analysis and dissemination), medium (more information needed) or low (no
  immediate action), often in bulk with IT tools. Operational analysis matches reports against lists, **links all
  reports about the same entity by name, date of birth, ID or passport number and address components**, and builds
  relationship clusters from shared surnames, phone numbers and addresses. Variations in spelling across reports are
  named as the main obstacle to linking. Strategic analysis produces typologies, trends, patterns and risk assessments.
- **Power to obtain information from reporting entities** (FATF INR.29): what an FIU can demand from a bank, by
  analytical need — identification data of natural persons (ID document data, date and place of birth, address);
  legal persons (name, legal form, proof of existence, articles, senior managers, registered office and principal place
  of business); beneficial owners (ownership, control by other means, senior managing official; trust parties);
  purpose and intended nature of the relationship; persons acting on the customer's behalf and their authority;
  **source of wealth and funds of PEPs and their family members**; wire originator and beneficiary data; bank account
  information; business correspondence; and **findings of the bank's own analysis** (e.g. inquiries into complex,
  unusual large transactions).
- **Annex C, improving STR quality (lessons of the Egmont ISIL project on terrorist financing)**: judge STRs by
  **volume, timeliness and relevance**; keep formats consistent and promote electronic reporting; make sure STRs
  capture all relevant information, including **geolocation data and unique identifiers** that law enforcement can link:
  **IP addresses when accounts are used online (especially from abroad), telephone numbers and e-mail addresses**.
  FIUs should share TF indicators and terrorist characteristics with at-risk sectors through meetings, dedicated
  contacts, alerts and advisories.
- The ISIL project also found FIUs blocked from sharing when an investigation or a mutual legal assistance request
  was under way; the FATF INR.40 says this must not be grounds for refusal.

What it means for AKR-TMS (section 3 as a whole):
- **FIU and authority request register.** Every request from the Office (whether domestic, or a query run on behalf of a
  foreign FIU under Egmont principle 18) is logged with its goAML reference, received date, urgency, deadline, subjects
  and requested items. A **response package builder** assembles the Egmont/INR.29 item list from the CBS replica and
  AKR-TMS records: identity data and ID images from the evidence vault, legal-entity documents, UBO chain, account list
  and statements for the requested period, wire originator/beneficiary data, PEP SoW/SoF records, prior alerts, cases
  and the bank's analysis. Same register for requests from courts, the Integrity Commission (Nazaha), the CBI and law
  enforcement, with a category per requester. Deadlines are tracked so the Office can meet its 30-business-day
  Egmont target.
- **Suspension and freeze orders with verification.** AKR-TMS is read-only on the CBS, so it cannot freeze anything
  itself. It records the order (issuer, legal basis, subjects, accounts, time received), creates a task for operations
  to apply the block in the CBS, and then **verifies from the read-only replica** that the block flag is set and that no
  debit has posted since the order time; any breach raises a critical alert to the MLRO. The time from order to
  confirmed block is reported as a KPI (the same mechanism serves UN freezes "without delay", section 4).
- **STRs built for the FIU's entity resolution.** Every STR export carries structured identifiers: national ID,
  passport and residence numbers, date and place of birth, mother's name (used on Iraqi lists), all known name
  spellings in Arabic and Latin script, addresses, phone numbers, e-mail addresses, account numbers and IBANs,
  counterparties, and, where the digital channel database is linked (section 2q), **IP addresses and device
  identifiers**, flagged when foreign. Pre-submission checks block exports with missing key identifiers. This applies the
  Egmont ISIL-project lessons directly and suits the goAML schema (file 23).
- **Timeliness KPI**: time from first alert to STR filing, and from case escalation to filing, per case and per branch.
- **FIU feedback register** (already planned): record the Office's feedback per report and per typology, feed STR
  usefulness indicators (section 2m) and tuning.
- Report types beyond STRs: CTRs at USD 10,000 (goAML) and TFS application reports (match and frozen-amount reports to
  the Committee for Freezing Terrorist Funds through the IQTFS platform, file 01 section 6, plus an STR to the Office),
  generated from the replica and queued for submission.

Legal frame for banks: UN Security Council decisions under Chapter VII bind Iraq as a UN member. FATF R.6 (terrorism)

### 4c. What "freezing without delay" requires in practice (UN measures + FATF INR.6/7, file 03)
- Freeze all funds and other assets owned or controlled, wholly or jointly, directly or indirectly, by designated
  persons, by persons acting on their behalf, and by entities they own or control, including derived income; no prior
  notice to the customer.
- Prohibit making funds or services available to them (outgoing payments, new products, loans, payouts to third parties
  for their benefit).
- Report frozen assets and attempted transactions to the competent authority (CFTF through IQTFS, plus an STR to the
  Office, file 01).
- Protect bona fide third parties; apply authorised exemptions (e.g. basic expenses) only with the committee or
  national authority's approval; unfreeze promptly after delisting or once a false positive is verified.
- Re-screen the whole customer base and pending payments **on every list change** (BCBS 2020, section 1e; Wolfsberg
  2019, section 2d).

### 4d. What it means for AKR-TMS
- **List ingestion service for the UN feed**: follow the redirect to the signed URL on every poll (never store the
  signed URL), validate against the XSD, compute a content hash, and **diff against the previous version** by DATAID and
  REFERENCE_NUMBER to produce additions, amendments (VERSIONNUM up) and removals. Poll at least hourly (the CBI minimum
  is 12 hours); also trigger an immediate poll when the UN e-mail update or committee press-release feed announces a
  change. Store every version with its hash so any past screening decision can be replayed against the list as it was.
- **Alert on schema change**: the UN currently serves a file it calls "legacy"; the parser must fail loudly (and fall
  back to the last good version) if the structure changes, and the product team must track the UN's new data model.
- **Normalisation layer**: map alias QUALITY values (Good / Low / "a.k.a." / "f.k.a." / empty) to strong, weak and
  former aliases; **weak (Low quality) aliases never alert on their own** but add weight when other identifiers match
  (UN definition plus Wolfsberg 2019, section 2d); normalise DOB types (exact, approximate, year-only, year range) into
  match tolerances; clean document types and numbers so passport and national ID numbers can match CBS ID fields
  exactly; keep the four name parts for patronymic-aware Arabic matching; use NAME_ORIGINAL_SCRIPT for direct Arabic
  matching.
- **Unified watchlist model** across UN, IQTFS national list, OFAC, EU, UK and internal lists, with source, regime and
  measure per entry (freeze vs travel ban vs vigilance), cross-list de-duplication, and an "effective from" time for
  each entry so the system can prove what it knew and when.
- **Delta re-screening on every change**: new or amended entries are screened against all customers, beneficial owners,
  signatories, counterparties seen in recent payments and pending items; removals trigger a review of existing freezes
  and open alerts (possible unfreeze). The time from UN publication to completed re-screen is a KPI shown on the
  sanctions dashboard and in the CBDDQ evidence pack (section 2c: business days to load list updates).
- **Freeze workflow with read-only verification** (shared with section 3): on a confirmed match the system records the
  decision, notifies operations to block the accounts in the CBS, verifies from the replica that the block is in place
  and that nothing moved after the match time, prepares the CFTF/IQTFS match report with frozen amounts and the STR,
  and tracks exemptions and delistings.
- **1518 module for Iraq**: treat the former-regime list as a domestic list with relative detection (immediate family
  is in scope; patronymic and tribal-name logic from section 2n, always confirmed by a human), screen entities owned or
  controlled by listed persons (UBO chains), and record the transfer instructions received from the Iraqi authorities.
- **Iran proliferation pack**: screening of the 121 restored designations plus ownership and control (50 percent and
  control tests for entities), an **"Iran nexus" vigilance flag** (Iranian-incorporated counterparties, Iranian
  addresses, IRGC- or IRISL-linked names, vessels and IMO numbers in trade documents, Iranian banks in payment chains),
  and PF scenarios: dual-use goods descriptions in LC and invoice text, trade routed through third countries, front
  companies with Iranian owners, payments for shipping or insurance linked to listed vessels. These map to the CBI 2025
  PF indicators (file 01) and FATF INR.7 (file 03).
- **Audit trail for regulators and correspondents**: for each list version, when it was published, when AKR-TMS loaded
  it, how many customers and payments were re-screened, how many alerts resulted and how they were closed.
